\section{Notation and the outcome table}
\label{sec:notation}

The \emph{audit set} of the main text is a labelled random sample of the screened library used neither for training nor for calibration.

Labels are $y\in\{0,1\}$: 1 for the in-class compounds, those with the property the screen looks for (toxic, soluble, or reaching the conductivity target), and 0 for the out-class compounds. The trained model gives predicted probabilities $\hat p(y\mid x)$ and nonconformity scores $s(x,y)=1-\hat p(y\mid x)$. For class $y$ let $S^{(y)}_{(1)}\le\dots\le S^{(y)}_{(n_y)}$ be the sorted scores of the $n_y$ calibration compounds of class $y$, and $\tau_y=S^{(y)}_{(k_y)}$. The prediction set is $C(x)=\{y: s(x,y)\le\tau_y\}$, one of $\{0\}$, $\{1\}$, $\{0,1\}$, $\varnothing$.

For a fixed pair of thresholds, write $c\in\{\{0\},\{1\},\{0,1\},\varnothing\}$ for the prediction set and
\begin{equation}
p_{y,c}=\Pr\big(Y=y,\ C(X)=c\big)
\end{equation}
for the probability of a cell of the outcome table (main text Table~\ref{main-tab:outcomes}) when compounds are independent. Under exchangeability we read $p_{y,c}$ as $p_{y,c}(Q)$, the long-run frequency of the cell given the de Finetti distribution $Q$ and the thresholds (Section~\ref{sec:intervals}). Any screening quantity is
\begin{equation}
p_g=\sum_{y,c} g(y,c)\,p_{y,c},\qquad g(y,c)\in\{0,1\},
\end{equation}
for a fixed indicator $g$ that selects cells. Examples: missed in-class compounds, $g=\mathbf 1\{y=1,c=\{0\}\}$; false leads, $g=\mathbf 1\{y=0,c=\{1\}\}$; deferred, $g=\mathbf 1\{c\in\{\{0,1\},\varnothing\}\}$; coverage, $g=\mathbf 1\{y\in c\}$. Ratios of such sums, for example the fraction of in-class compounds among compounds labelled $\{1\}$, follow directly. The same identity holds for counts: if $K_{y,c}$ is the number of test compounds in cell $(y,c)$, the count of quantity $g$ is $K_g=\sum_{y,c}g(y,c)K_{y,c}$. Within each class the cells sum to the class total, $\sum_c p_{y,c}=\Pr(Y=y)$.

\section{Coverage given the calibration set and the finite-sample correction}
\label{sec:correction}

\paragraph{Exact laws.} Consider one class and drop the index $y$; let there be $n$ calibration scores and $\tau=S_{(k)}$, $u=n+1-k$; in this section $u$ denotes this grid index, not the predicted probability. Scores are assumed continuous. With tied scores, randomized tie-breaking restores all laws in this section exactly: every compound, calibration or future, receives an independent uniform number, and compounds are ordered by score and then by that number. This needs no knowledge of future scores. Our scripts implement it equivalently, on stored scores, by adding a uniform jitter smaller than the smallest gap between distinct scores. Without it, ties keep the one-sided coverage statement conservative, but they can break the two-sided count intervals of Sections~\ref{sec:intervals} and \ref{sec:calonly}: a cluster of tied scores at a threshold moves together, so the upper limit for the number of future compounds at or below the threshold can be exceeded (Section~\ref{sec:sim}, Table~\ref{tab:ties}). We therefore break ties at random in all molecular analyses. For a future batch of $m$ compounds of this class, the number whose score is at most $\tau$ depends only on the ranks of the $n+m$ exchangeable scores, all orderings of which are equally likely. Its distribution is therefore
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{BetaBinomial}(m;\,k,\,u),
\end{equation}
exactly and for any exchangeable data.\citep{Marques2025Beta} If compounds are independent with score distribution $F$, the coverage of the deployed model is $F(S_{(k)})$. This is the $k$-th order statistic of $n$ independent uniform variables, so
\begin{equation}
F(S_{(k)})\sim\mathrm{Beta}(k,\,n+1-k).
\end{equation}
This is Vovk's training-conditional result.\citep{Vovk2012PAC,Bian2023TrainingConditional} Under infinite exchangeability the same Beta law holds conditional on the de Finetti directing distribution, and therefore also marginally (Section~\ref{sec:intervals}).

\paragraph{Correction.} For a request $(\alpha,\delta)$ and batch size $m$, let $x^\star=\lceil(1-\alpha)m\rceil$, computed in integer arithmetic. The correction selects the least conservative order statistic, that is the largest $u$, such that
\begin{equation}
\Pr\big(X\ge x^\star\big)\ge 1-\delta,\qquad X\sim\mathrm{BetaBinomial}(m;\,n+1-u,\,u).
\end{equation}
The batch-free version replaces the Beta--Binomial by $\mathrm{Beta}(n+1-u,u)$ and the event by $\{Z\ge1-\alpha\}$. The procedure is applied separately to each class; for class $y$, $m$ is the number of future compounds of that class, and the guarantee holds conditional on the class labels.

\paragraph{Algorithm.} (i) Set $x^\star=\lceil(1-\alpha)m\rceil$. (ii) For $u=1,\dots,n$ compute the upper-tail probability above. (iii) Return the largest $u$ for which it is at least $1-\delta$, together with $k=n+1-u$ and the deployed level $u/(n+1)$; if there is none, return \emph{infeasible}.

\paragraph{Feasibility.} The most conservative choice $u=1$ gives coverage distributed as $\mathrm{Beta}(n,1)$. A batch-free request is therefore feasible if and only if
\begin{equation}
\alpha\ge1-\delta^{1/n}.
\end{equation}
A batch request is feasible if and only if $\Pr(X\ge x^\star)\ge1-\delta$ for $X\sim\mathrm{BetaBinomial}(m;\,n,\,1)$.
Distinct requests often select the same $u$ and hence the same deployed model (Fig.~\ref{fig:map}).

\paragraph{Baselines.} The standard choice is $k=\lceil(n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil$. The DKW-based correction replaces $\alpha$ by $\alpha-\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/(2n)}$ (one-sided bound with the Massart constant),\citep{Massart1990DKWM} and falls back to $k=n$ when this value is not positive.

\section{Prediction intervals for future-batch counts from an audit set}
\label{sec:intervals}

\paragraph{Construction.} Fix the calibration set, hence the thresholds and the prediction-set map. For a fixed indicator $g$, let $I_1,\dots,I_n$ be its values on the test compounds and $I_{n+1},\dots,I_{n+m}$ on the future batch. The audit and future compounds are exchangeable with one another given the calibration set, which is what this construction needs and all that it needs: the calibration set enters only through the thresholds it fixed, so the requirement is conditional on it and it need not be exchangeable with the audit or the batch. Nor need it be independent of them: a random division of a fixed library makes the three parts dependent through the library's finite size, and the construction is valid there, as the fixed-library simulation below confirms. Given the calibration set the $n+m$ indicators are therefore exchangeable. Let $K=\sum_{i\le n}I_i$, $K_m=\sum_{i>n}I_i$ and $T=K+K_m$. Conditional on $T=t$, every placement of the $t$ ones among the $n+m$ positions is equally likely, so
\begin{equation}
K\mid T=t\ \sim\ \mathrm{Hypergeometric}(n+m,\,t,\,n),
\end{equation}
whatever the distribution of the data. For each candidate $j$ for $K_m$, test $T=K+j$ with this distribution and keep $j$ if neither one-sided test rejects at level $\gamma/2$. The accepted values form an interval $[L,U]$ with $\Pr(L\le K_m\le U)\ge1-\gamma$.

\paragraph{Closed form.} For integers $0\le k\le n$ and $0\le j\le m$,
\begin{equation}
\Pr\big(K\ge k\mid T=k+j\big)=\Pr\big(B\le j\big),\qquad B\sim\mathrm{BetaBinomial}(m;\,k,\,n-k+1),
\end{equation}
and symmetrically for the other tail with parameters $(k+1,\,n-k)$.\citep{Thatcher1964} Hence
\begin{equation}
L=\min\{j:\ F_{\mathrm{BB}(m;k,n-k+1)}(j)>\gamma/2\},\qquad
U=\text{the }1-\gamma/2\text{ quantile of }\mathrm{BetaBinomial}(m;\,k+1,\,n-k),
\end{equation}
with $L=0$ if $k=0$ and $U=m$ if $k=n$. We verified both identities numerically for $n\in\{20,57,200\}$, $m\in\{10,100,1000\}$ and all $k$ and $j$; the largest discrepancy was $6\times10^{-13}$ (script in Section~\ref{sec:software}). The interval $[L,U]$ reaches at least $1-\gamma$ even conditional on each total $T$. It contains the Bayesian posterior predictive intervals with priors $\mathrm{Beta}(1,1)$ and $\mathrm{Beta}(1/2,1/2)$, so their coverage is never higher than that of the exact interval.

\paragraph{Sizing the audit set.} For planning, the exact limits are close to a normal approximation: for an outcome of frequency $p$, $n$ audit compounds and a batch of $m$, the 95\% interval for the future count is about $mp\pm2m\sqrt{p(1-p)(1/n+1/m)}$. The term $1/n$ is the uncertainty of the audited frequency and $1/m$ the variation of a finite batch; the second does not vanish however large the audit. For AqSolDB setting A ($p=0.23$ false leads, $n=384$, $m=1000$) the approximation gives $232\pm51$, against the exact $[183,285]$. Joint bounds over $J$ settings and $C$ outcomes replace the factor 2 by the corresponding normal quantile, about 3.8 for $JC=576$ and $\varepsilon=0.05$.

Inverting the approximation sizes the audit. Letting the audit grow without limit leaves the half-width at $h_\infty=2\sqrt{m\,p(1-p)}$, which is the narrowest interval any audit can buy for a batch of $m$, and writing the target half-width as $r\,h_\infty$ for some $r>1$ gives
\begin{equation}
n = \frac{m}{r^2-1}.
\label{eq:auditsize}
\end{equation}
The rate and the batch size cancel, so where a design sits above its floor is fixed by $n/m$ alone: $n=m/3$ gives twice the floor, $n=m$ a factor $\sqrt2$, $n=2.3m$ a factor 1.2, $n=4.8m$ a factor 1.1 and $n\approx10m$ a factor 1.05. Checked against the exact limits at $p$ from 0.02 to 0.50 and $m$ from 96 to 1000, the realized ratio matches $r$ to within a few percent, the discrepancies being those of discreteness at small expected counts. The floor does depend on the rate, and relative to the expected count $mp$ it is $2\sqrt{(1-p)/(pm)}$, which is 44\% at $p=0.02$ and $m=1000$ against 9\% at $p=0.5$ and $m=500$: at the rates of a primary screen the batch term dominates, and a larger audit cannot repair it.

\paragraph{Why the exact limits are used.} The decomposition above is the ordinary large-sample one. Treating the candidates as independent draws at rate $p$ and the audited frequency as $\hat p=K_n/n$, the prediction error of $m\hat p$ has variance $mp(1-p)+m^2p(1-p)/n$, which is $m^2p(1-p)(1/n+1/m)$: the first term is the batch and the second the estimate. Equation~\ref{eq:auditsize} is exact in that limit rather than an approximation to it, since the ratio of half-widths is $\sqrt{1+m/n}$ by construction. None of this is new, and the exact construction we use is its finite-population counterpart --- predicting the defectives in the remainder of a lot from a sample of it\citep{Thatcher1964,Wilks1941,DodgeRomig1944} --- so the two are descriptions of one quantity.

They do not agree where screens operate, which is why the exact limits are used throughout. The sizing figures quoted in this paragraph and in the main text invert a \emph{one-sided} lower limit, placing the whole of $\gamma=0.05$ in the lower tail rather than $\gamma/2$ in each tail as the two-sided limits above do; the one-sided form is the right object for a question about a shortfall only. The two conventions differ by a factor that grows as the audit shrinks --- about 1.1 at an audit of 1000, 1.2 at 384, and 2.0 at 49 --- so the convention must be read with the figure. Because $\gamma$ denotes a one-sided level in the sizing figures and a two-sided one elsewhere, we write $\gamma_1$ for the former where both appear. Sizing a round for 20 hits, the large-sample rule and the exact limits agree to within 3\% whenever the audit has seen about twenty in-class compounds or more: at $p=0.5$ for every audit size, at $p=0.10$ from $n=384$ (38 seen) and at $p=0.02$ from $n=1000$ (20 seen). Below that the large-sample rule is conservative and then wrong. At $p=0.02$ with $n=384$, 8 in-class compounds seen, it asks for 2690 candidates against the exact 2056, and at $p=0.10$ with $n=49$, 5 seen, 736 against 510. At $p=0.02$ with $n=49$, one in-class compound seen, it returns no finite batch at all: the lower bound it places on the rate is not positive, so no batch size makes the bound reach the target. The rule needs $n>z^2(1-p)/p$ audit candidates before it will admit any finite answer, about 133 at a rate of 2\%, whereas the exact limits return 19\,091. A team following the approximation at a primary screen's hit rate would therefore over-size its rounds, and on a small audit would abandon a plan that is in fact feasible.

\paragraph{Target of the interval.} The interval covers the realized future count $K_m$; no fixed rate is assumed. If the compound source is regarded as infinitely exchangeable, de Finetti's theorem\citep{Aldous1985} gives a random distribution $Q$ such that, conditional on $Q$, compounds are independent draws from $Q$. Conditional on $Q$ and the calibration set the test indicators are then independent Bernoulli variables with success probability
\begin{equation}
p_g(Q)=\Pr_Q\big(g(Y,C(X))=1\big),
\end{equation}
so $K$ is Binomial and the Clopper--Pearson interval covers $p_g(Q)$ with probability at least $1-\gamma$, conditionally and therefore marginally. $p_g(Q)$ is the long-run frequency of the outcome in this deployment. The probability $\Pr(g=1\mid\Dcal)$, averaged over $Q$, is not the success probability of independent trials, and intervals for it are not valid in general; in the two-stage simulation of Table~\ref{tab:twostage} Clopper--Pearson coverage of that quantity fell to 73\%. Finite exchangeable sequences need not be mixtures of independent sequences;\citep{DiaconisFreedman1980} the hypergeometric construction above does not need this representation.

\paragraph{Several outcomes.} Each interval is valid for one indicator fixed before seeing the audit set. For $J$ indicators, intervals at level $\gamma/J$ hold simultaneously. A joint region for the full outcome table can be obtained from the multivariate hypergeometric distribution of the cell counts given their totals.

\section{Prediction intervals from the calibration set alone}
\label{sec:calonly}

\paragraph{Events.} Use the notation of Section~\ref{sec:geometry}: $u=\hat p(1\mid x)$, $0\in C(x)$ iff $u\le\tau_0$, and $1\in C(x)$ iff $u\ge1-\tau_1$. Here $\tau_0$ is the $k_0$-th smallest of the $n_0$ out-class calibration scores $u$, and $\tau_1$ is the $k_1$-th smallest of the $n_1$ in-class calibration scores $1-u$. For out-class compounds define
\begin{equation}
A_0=\{u\le\tau_0\}\ \text{(coverage)},\qquad B_0=\{u\ge1-\tau_1\}\ \text{(labelled in-class)},
\end{equation}
and for in-class compounds $A_1=\{u\ge1-\tau_1\}$ and $B_1=\{u\le\tau_0\}$.

\paragraph{Own-class events.} Let $m_y$ be the number of future compounds of class $y$. The number of them in $A_y$ is the number of $m_y$ exchangeable scores at or below the $k_y$-th of $n_y$ calibration scores. Its distribution is exactly\citep{Marques2025Beta}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{BetaBinomial}(m_y;\,k_y,\,n_y+1-k_y),
\end{equation}
and its equal-tailed quantiles give an interval.

\paragraph{Cross-class events.} $B_0$ depends on the data only through $\tau_1$, which is a function of the in-class calibration compounds. Given the labels and the in-class calibration compounds, the out-class calibration compounds and the future out-class compounds are exchangeable. Let $c_0$ be the number of out-class calibration compounds in $B_0$. The audit-set construction of Section~\ref{sec:intervals}, with $n=n_0$, $K=c_0$ and $m=m_0$, then gives an interval for the number of future out-class compounds in $B_0$. The same holds for $B_1$ with the roles of the classes exchanged.

\paragraph{Cells.} By Proposition~\ref{prop:regimes}, if $\tau_0+\tau_1\ge1$ then $A_0^c\subseteq B_0$, and the out-class row is
\begin{equation}
\{1\}=A_0^c,\qquad \{0\}=B_0^c,\qquad \text{both}=A_0\cap B_0,\qquad \varnothing=\emptyset .
\end{equation}
If $\tau_0+\tau_1<1$ then $A_0\cap B_0=\emptyset$, and
\begin{equation}
\{0\}=A_0,\qquad \{1\}=B_0,\qquad \varnothing=A_0^c\cap B_0^c,\qquad \text{both}=\emptyset .
\end{equation}
The regime is known once the thresholds are set, but it depends on the calibration data of both classes, so a cell's identity with one event or the other is data dependent. We therefore compute the interval for $|A_0|$ (rank law) and the interval for $|B_0|$ (cross-class) each at level $1-\gamma/2$. Both then hold simultaneously with probability at least $1-\gamma$, whatever the regime. Every cell of the row is bounded by arithmetic on the two: in the two-label regime, $\{1\}$ and $\{0\}$ are bounded by the complements of $A_0$ and $B_0$, and the both-labels cell by $|A_0|+|B_0|-m_0$; in the empty regime, $\{0\}$ and $\{1\}$ are bounded by $A_0$ and $B_0$, and the empty cell by $m_0-|A_0|-|B_0|$. The intervals for all cells of the row hold jointly at level $1-\gamma$. The in-class row is analogous: missed in-class compounds are $A_1^c$ (rank law) and confident hits are $B_1^c$ (cross-class) in the two-label regime.

\paragraph{Mixed batches.} The guarantees above are conditional on $m_y$. For a batch of $m$ compounds of unknown composition, the number of in-class compounds $m_1$ has the audit-set interval from the calibration class counts ($K=n_1$ out of $n_0+n_1$). We compute the interval for $m_1$ at level $1-\gamma/2$, and each of the four event intervals ($A_0,B_0,A_1,B_1$) at level $1-\gamma/8$, for every $m_1$ in that interval, and take the union over $m_1$. By the union bound the five statements hold together with probability at least $1-\gamma$, so the intervals for all cells of the mixed batch hold jointly at level $1-\gamma$. The union over $m_1$ is conservative; mixing the class-wise laws over the exact distribution of $m_1$ would give shorter intervals.

\paragraph{When it does not apply.} If scores are not class probabilities ($s(x,0)+s(x,1)\ne1$), the sets $A_0\cap B_0$ and $A_0^c\cap B_0^c$ can both have positive probability. Their counts then depend on the joint behaviour of two thresholds on the same compounds, and an audit set is needed. Evaluating a class's own threshold on the compounds that set it, beyond the rank law, is not allowed (Section~\ref{sec:reuse}).

\section{Same-class reuse of the calibration set, and the leave-one-out estimate}
\label{sec:reuse}

Let $I_i(t)=\mathbf 1\{S_i\le t\}$ for the $n$ calibration scores of one class. If $\tau=S_{(k)}=t$, then exactly $k$ indicators equal one, so the indicators are not independent given the threshold:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Cov}\big(I_i(t),I_j(t)\mid\tau=t\big)=-\frac{k(n-k)}{n^2(n-1)}<0,\qquad i\ne j,\ k<n.
\end{equation}
Outcome counts that evaluate a class's own threshold on the calibration compounds of that class inherit this dependence and are not exchangeable with future counts; only the rank law of Section~\ref{sec:correction} describes them exactly. Cross-class events are not affected (Section~\ref{sec:calonly}). Intervals built from them are too narrow, and their centres can also be biased near thresholds.

\paragraph{Leave-one-out estimate.} When no audit set is available, each calibration compound $i$ can be assigned to a prediction set using thresholds recomputed without it. The pooled cell counts give a leave-one-out estimate of the outcome table.\citep{vovk2015crossconformal,barber2021predictive} The thresholds change from one compound to the next, so this estimate carries no guarantee. With class probabilities, the calibration-only intervals of Section~\ref{sec:calonly} make it unnecessary; for other scores it is useful only for exploring settings before an audit set is collected.

\section{Binary prediction sets with probability scores}
\label{sec:geometry}

Write $u=s(x,0)=\hat p(1\mid x)$. If $\hat p(0\mid x)+\hat p(1\mid x)=1$ then $s(x,1)=1-u$, and the sets are determined by $u$ alone: $0\in C(x)$ iff $u\le\tau_0$ and $1\in C(x)$ iff $u\ge1-\tau_1$.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:regimes}
With probability scores, (i) the two-label set has positive probability only if $\tau_0+\tau_1>1$, in which case $u\in[1-\tau_1,\tau_0]$; (ii) the empty set has positive probability only if $\tau_0+\tau_1<1$, in which case $u\in(\tau_0,1-\tau_1)$; (iii) if $\tau_0+\tau_1=1$ (and $u$ has a continuous distribution) only single-label sets occur.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Direct from the two conditions on $u$.
\end{proof}

When $\tau_0+\tau_1>1$, compounds with $u<1-\tau_1$ receive $\{0\}$, those with $u>\tau_0$ receive $\{1\}$, and those in between receive both labels (main text Fig.~\ref{main-fig:sets}). Raising $\tau_1$ moves compounds from $\{0\}$ to both labels; raising $\tau_0$ moves compounds from $\{1\}$ to both labels. Each single-label set is thus controlled by the threshold of the other class. This is why changing the request for one class can change the outcomes of the other.

\section{Cost ratios for which acting on single labels is cheapest}
\label{sec:costs}

Consider actions: label 0, label 1, or defer, with costs $c_{\text{miss}}$ for assigning 0 to a compound of class 1, $c_{\text{lead}}$ for assigning 1 to a compound of class 0, $c_{\text{defer}}$ for deferring, and zero for correct labels. For a prediction set $c$ with positive probability let
\begin{equation}
\eta_c=\Pr\big(Y=1\mid C(X)=c\big)=\frac{p_{1,c}}{p_{0,c}+p_{1,c}}.
\end{equation}
A rule that acts only on the prediction set has expected costs $\eta_c c_{\text{miss}}$ (label 0), $(1-\eta_c)c_{\text{lead}}$ (label 1) and $c_{\text{defer}}$ (defer). With $\lambda=c_{\text{miss}}/c_{\text{lead}}$ and $\rho=c_{\text{defer}}/c_{\text{lead}}$, the rule ``$\{1\}\to$ label 1, $\{0\}\to$ label 0, otherwise defer'' minimizes expected cost if and only if all of the following hold for the sets that occur:\citep{Chow1970}
\begin{equation}
\eta_{\{1\}}\ge\max\Big\{\frac{1}{1+\lambda},\,1-\rho\Big\},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\eta_{\{0\}}\le\min\Big\{\frac{1}{1+\lambda},\,\frac{\rho}{\lambda}\Big\},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\frac{\rho}{\lambda}\le\eta_c\le1-\rho\quad\text{for } c\in\{\{0,1\},\varnothing\}.
\end{equation}
Each condition is linear in $(\lambda,\rho)$, so the admissible region is a polygon, possibly empty. Deferral can be optimal only if $c_{\text{defer}}\le c_{\text{miss}}c_{\text{lead}}/(c_{\text{miss}}+c_{\text{lead}})$. The $\eta_c$ are estimated from the outcome table.

\paragraph{Set-based rules contain Chow's rule.} Let $\eta(u)=\Pr(Y=1\mid u)$, and suppose $\eta$ is nondecreasing in $u$, which is an assumption about the model and not a consequence of its ranking well; Table~\ref{tab:tox21mono} shows it failing on nine of twelve endpoints. In units of $c_{\text{lead}}$, the expected costs of the three actions for a compound with score $u$ are $\lambda\eta(u)$ (label 0), $1-\eta(u)$ (label 1) and $\rho$ (defer). Chow's optimal rule\citep{Chow1970} takes the cheapest action at each $u$. Because $\eta$ is monotone, the optimal rule labels 0 for $u<l$, defers for $l\le u\le h$ and labels 1 for $u>h$, for some $l\le h$. The set-based rule with $\tau_0=h$ and $\tau_1=1-l$ produces exactly this partition (Section~\ref{sec:geometry}). If deferral is never optimal, then $l=h$ and the thresholds satisfy $\tau_0+\tau_1=1$. Conformal thresholds are calibration order statistics, so $(l,h)$ can be matched only up to the spacing of the calibration scores. In addition, a sweep over a finite grid of requests reaches only the threshold pairs on that grid. Choosing the request by cost therefore loses nothing relative to Chow's rule beyond the resolution of the calibration scores and of the request grid, \emph{provided} $\eta$ is monotone. Requiring coverage of at least $1-\alpha$ for both classes restricts $(l,h)$, and its cost can be computed (Table~\ref{tab:cost}). The argument needs $\eta$ to be monotone, not calibrated: the set-based rule uses only the ordering of $u$. If $\eta$ is not monotone, Chow's rule on calibrated probabilities is not a two-threshold rule on $u$ and can be cheaper; Table~\ref{tab:tox21mono} gives a monotonicity diagnostic for the Tox21 models. The containment is a statement about population rules. On finite data, a set rule chosen from calibration estimates and Chow's rule on fitted probabilities are both estimates, and either can be cheaper (Table~\ref{tab:chow}). What the set rule adds is the interval guarantee for its outcome counts, which after selection holds only with the correction of the Selection paragraph below.

\paragraph{Uncertainty in $\eta_c$.} The conditions above use estimated within-set fractions $\hat\eta_c$. A robust region is the set of $(\lambda,\rho)$ for which the conditions hold for every $\eta_c$ in simultaneous Clopper--Pearson intervals: Bonferroni over the sets that occur, computed from the counts $p_{0,c},p_{1,c}$ of the outcome table. The conditions are monotone in each $\eta_c$, so this needs only the interval endpoints. These intervals need the joint counts of label and prediction set on compounds not used to set the thresholds, that is a labelled audit set; the calibration-only intervals of Section~\ref{sec:calonly} bound the cells of each row separately and do not by themselves give intervals for the within-set fractions. In the solubility example the robust region therefore uses the audit set.

\paragraph{Selection.} A sweep over a grid of $J$ requests yields at most $J$ distinct rules. If intervals for all rules are computed at level $\gamma/J$, they hold simultaneously with probability at least $1-\gamma$, so the intervals of any rule chosen by looking at them remain valid. Choosing by an estimated cost or by a capacity constraint is then safe. We divide by the size of the request grid, which is fixed in advance; dividing by the number of distinct rules is also valid when the thresholds are set from data independent of the data used for the intervals (as in the audit-set design), because the set of distinct rules is then fixed given the calibration set. Bonferroni is conservative when $J$ is large. Sequential procedures such as fixed-sequence testing\citep{Angelopoulos2021LTT} control error for hypotheses about fixed parameters, for example the long-run deferral rate $p_g(Q)$ of Section~\ref{sec:intervals}; they do not by themselves protect statements about realized future counts, because which settings exceed a capacity in the future batch is itself random. We therefore use Bonferroni for all statements about future counts.

\paragraph{The usual set-to-action rule is max--min optimal.} Measure costs in units of a false lead: a miss costs $\lambda$, a deferral $\rho$, a correct call 0. Kiyani \textit{et al.}\ show that a decision maker who knows only a prediction set with marginal coverage, and wants a high-probability guarantee on its utility, should take the action with the best worst case over the labels in the set.\citep{Kiyani2025RAC} For $\{1\}$ the worst-case cost of labelling 1 is 0, so the rule labels 1; likewise for $\{0\}$. For $\{0,1\}$ the worst cases are $\lambda$ (label 0), 1 (label 1) and $\rho$ (defer), so the rule defers whenever $\rho\le\min(\lambda,1)$. Empty sets occur here by design rather than only on miscoverage; we defer them. Acting on single labels and deferring the rest is therefore the max--min action for every cost with $\rho\le\min(\lambda,1)$, whatever the coverage setting. Expected-cost (Chow) optimality, the criterion that governs the average over a campaign, holds only where the within-set fractions satisfy the conditions above; the two criteria answer different questions. Kiyani \textit{et al.}\ also show that, for a given utility, the optimal prediction sets are level sets of utility rather than of a score, so class-conditional threshold sets are not in general the optimal sets.

\paragraph{Scenario maps (main text Fig.~\ref{main-fig:solubility}, Fig.~\ref{fig:calisolscen}).} For one split, each request on the $12\times12$ grid ($\delta=0.10$, batch correction with planned class counts) sets thresholds on the calibration set. For a future batch of $M=1000$, the audit set gives the point estimate of each outcome count and a one-sided bound from the construction of Section~\ref{sec:intervals}: upper bounds for deferrals (measurements), false leads and missed in-class compounds, a lower bound for confirmed in-class compounds. Each bound is computed at level $\varepsilon/(JC)$ with $\varepsilon=0.05$, $J=144$ requests and $C=4$ bounded outcomes ($8.7\times10^{-5}$), so that all bounds of all requests hold together with probability at least 95\%. A scenario lists limits on some of these bounds; a request is feasible if all its bounds satisfy the limits, and the feasible request with the fewest expected false leads is chosen. Because every bound of every request holds together, the chosen setting satisfies its constraints with probability at least 95\% although it was chosen by looking at the audit set. The CALiSol-23 maps use the first random split of the electrolyte screen. If the objective and the limits are monotone in the outcomes, an optimal feasible setting lies on the Pareto front of the vectors (upper bounds on deferrals, false leads and missed in-class compounds; lower bound on confirmed in-class compounds; and, for the point objective, expected false leads): if setting $b$ dominates setting $a$, then $b$ meets every limit $a$ meets and is at least as good under the objective. \section{Simulation protocols and additional results}
\label{sec:sim}

\paragraph{Threshold correction (Table~\ref{tab:grid}).} Calibration scores were drawn from the absolute value of a Cauchy distribution, and the future batch ($m=100$) from the same distribution, for $10^6$ independent calibration draws per $n$; all threshold rules were evaluated on the same draws.

\paragraph{Prediction intervals (main text Fig.~\ref{main-fig:validation}).} Each replicate drew a data distribution $Q$: prevalence $\pi\sim\mathrm{U}(0.05,0.5)$; predicted probability of being in-class $\mathrm{Beta}(a_1,b_1)$ for in-class compounds with $a_1\sim\mathrm U(2,10)$, $b_1\sim\mathrm U(2,4)$; and $\mathrm{Beta}(a_0,b_0)$ for out-class compounds with $a_0\sim\mathrm U(1.5,3)$, $b_0\sim\mathrm U(5,9)$. It then drew 1000 calibration compounds, $n$ test compounds and $m=1000$ future compounds independently from $Q$. Over replicates the data are exchangeable but not independent. Thresholds used the batch-free correction with $\alpha=\delta=0.10$ for each class. Replicates with fewer than 30 calibration compounds in a class were skipped (none occurred). The long-run cell probabilities given $Q$ and the thresholds were computed exactly from the Beta distribution functions. Besides the audit interval, three common alternatives were evaluated: the Bayesian posterior predictive with a uniform prior, Beta--Binomial$(m;K+1,n-K+1)$; the plug-in interval, Binomial$(m,K/n)$ quantiles; and $m$ times the Clopper--Pearson interval for the rate, rounded outward. Results are in Table~\ref{tab:validation}. Because the data are independent given $Q$, this design does not separate validity under exchangeability from validity under independence. The future count varies for two reasons: the audited rate is uncertain, which adds a variance of about $m^2p(1-p)/n$, and the batch itself is a sample, which adds about $mp(1-p)$. The plug-in interval keeps only the second term and is too narrow by a factor of about $\sqrt{1+m/n}$; $m$ times the Clopper--Pearson interval keeps only the first and is too narrow by about $\sqrt{1+n/m}$. Each fails where the term it omits dominates: the plug-in for small audits, the scaled Clopper--Pearson interval for audits as large as the batch, and for any audit when the batch is small. The audit interval accounts for both terms. Its limits use the same Beta parameters as the Clopper--Pearson limits but take Beta--Binomial rather than Beta quantiles, and as $m\to\infty$, $K_m/m$ recovers the Clopper--Pearson interval for the rate.

\paragraph{Calibration-only intervals (Fig.~\ref{fig:calonlyfig}, Table~\ref{tab:calonly}).} Data distributions as above; row-simultaneous intervals as described in Section~\ref{sec:calonly}; $n\in\{300,1000\}$ calibration compounds; batch-free correction with $\alpha=\delta=0.10$; future batch $m=1000$; 3000 replicates, of which those with fewer than 20 calibration compounds in a class were skipped (103 at $n=300$, none at $n=1000$). Class-conditional coverage was evaluated given the realized $m_y$, and mixed-batch coverage with the union over $m_1$. The comparison spends the same budget of $n$ labelled compounds half on calibration and half on an audit set, and computes audit-set intervals for the mixed batch.

\paragraph{Cost (main text Table~\ref{main-tab:cost}, Table~\ref{tab:cost}).} Exact population computation, with out-class scores $u\sim\mathrm{Beta}(2,7)$ and in-class scores $u\sim\mathrm{Beta}(a,b)$. The three settings are prevalence 0.15 with $(9,3)$ (well separated), prevalence 0.15 with $(4,3)$ (overlapping), and prevalence 0.5 with $(4,3)$ (balanced, overlapping). Thresholds $(l,h)$ were searched on a grid of 801 points. Chow: minimum over all $l\le h$. Constrained: minimum subject to class-wise coverage $\ge0.90$. Default: both coverages exactly 0.90.

\paragraph{Selection.} 4000 replicates. In each, prevalence $\sim\mathrm U(0.1,0.4)$, in-class compounds $\mathrm{Beta}(a,b)$ with $a\sim\mathrm U(3,9)$ and $b\sim\mathrm U(2,4)$, out-class compounds $\mathrm{Beta}(2,7)$. There were 500 calibration compounds, 200 test compounds and a future batch of 1000. The candidates were the rules from the standard thresholds at $\alpha_0,\alpha_1\in\{0.02,\dots,0.30\}$ (10 values each), de-duplicated to distinct threshold pairs (median 100, range 90--100). The rule chosen was the one with the fewest audit-set errors (missed in-class compounds plus false leads) among the rules whose one-sided upper limit for deferrals was at most 150. The level of this check was set either at $0.05$ for each rule (unadjusted) or at $0.05/J$ with $J$ the number of distinct rules (Bonferroni). For the chosen rule we recorded whether its realized future deferrals exceeded capacity, and whether its two-sided error interval, at the same per-rule level, covered its realized future errors. A rule was found in every replicate. Capacity was exceeded in 0.47\% of replicates (unadjusted) and 0.03\% (Bonferroni), against the nominal 5\%. The chosen rules made 43.5 and 52.5 future errors per batch of 1000. The error interval of the chosen rule covered the realized errors in 95.6\% (unadjusted) and 100\% (Bonferroni) of replicates. In this design the selection effect on the chosen rule's intervals is small, because neighbouring rules have strongly correlated counts; the unadjusted procedure carries no guarantee nonetheless.

\paragraph{Tied scores (Table~\ref{tab:ties}).} Out-class scores were drawn from $\mathrm{Beta}(2,7)$ and in-class scores from $\mathrm{Beta}(6,3)$, prevalence 0.2, with 1000 calibration and 1000 future compounds. A fraction $t\in\{0,4,6,10\}\%$ of out-class compounds, in both sets, received one common score at the 0.92 quantile of the out-class distribution. Thresholds were standard at $\alpha=0.10$, and the class-conditional calibration-only intervals of Section~\ref{sec:calonly} were evaluated with ties kept or broken at random. With ties kept, coverage of the correct-rejection count fell from 0.98 to 0.86, 0.81 and 0.71 for $t=4$, 6 and 10\%, and coverage of the whole out-class row to 0.84, 0.79 and 0.67. With randomized tie-breaking, coverage was at least 0.97 for every cell and at least 0.96 for each row. Tox21 out-class compounds have 3.2--4.4\% tied scores (Table~\ref{tab:batchlaw}).

\section{Molecular experiments}
\label{sec:molecular}

\paragraph{Data.} Tox21 was taken from the MoleculeNet file distributed with DeepChem, and AqSolDB from the curated set released by its authors;\citep{Sorkun2019AqSolDB} checksums are listed with the scripts. Molecules were parsed with RDKit,\citep{RDKit} reduced to the largest fragment, neutralized where possible and written as canonical SMILES; molecules that failed or had fewer than two heavy atoms were removed. For Tox21, 8014 records gave 7592 unique compounds. Duplicate labels were merged per endpoint, and 139 conflicting endpoint labels were set to missing. For AqSolDB, 9982 records gave 9399 unique compounds. Duplicates were averaged when their $\log S$ values differed by at most 0.5 log units and removed otherwise, leaving 9254 compounds, 2467 of them with RDKit $\log P>3.5$.

\paragraph{Features and models.} Morgan fingerprints\citep{Rogers2010Morgan} had radius 2 and 2048 bits. CatBoost\citep{Prokhorenkova2018CatBoost} used 500 trees, depth 6, learning rate 0.1 and default regularization; hyperparameters were fixed in advance and not tuned. There was no early stopping, and hence no tuning set. A single model class and representation were used throughout, because the comparisons in this paper are between ways of obtaining intervals for a given model, not between models.

\paragraph{Tox21 splits.} Each endpoint was split into training, calibration and audit sets in proportions 50/25/25, 50 times at random. It was also split 20 times by Bemis--Murcko scaffold.\citep{Bemis1996Murcko} For the scaffold splits, scaffolds were shuffled and each scaffold group was assigned to the set furthest below its target size; acyclic compounds were treated as singleton groups. Training and audit sets shared no scaffold. One model was fitted per split. Scores of calibration and test compounds were tie-broken at random before thresholds were set (Section~\ref{sec:correction}); a median of 3.3--4.3\% of scores per endpoint were tied, and in random splits a median of 3.5\% of test compounds (at most 4.8\%) had a fingerprint identical to one in the calibration set. Such duplicates do not break exchangeability under random splits, because which member of a pair falls on which side is itself random. Thresholds used $\alpha=\delta=0.10$ for both classes. The batch correction took $m_y$ from the calibration prevalence and the audit-set size, not from the realized test class counts. Standard, batch-free and one-sided DKW thresholds were computed on the same splits. Pilot samples of 200, 500 and 1000 compounds were drawn at random from the audit set of each scaffold split (1000 only when at least 1200 test compounds were available, giving 224 pilots); each pilot was used as the calibration set and the remaining test compounds as the screened batch. Pilots with fewer than five in-class compounds were skipped (23 of 240 of 200 compounds, 1 of 240 of 500, none of 1000). Seeds: $100000+1000j+r$ (random) and $100000+1000j+r+500$ (scaffold), for endpoint $j$ and repeat $r$. These runs replace those of the first version of this paper, which used other seeds and no tie-breaking; scores, labels and split indices of every run are stored.

\paragraph{Violations on real data.} For each split and class, the exact probability that per-class test coverage falls below 90\% follows from the batch law for the thresholds used and the realized test class count (Section~\ref{sec:correction}). Summing these probabilities gives the expected number of violations, and the exact distribution of the number of violations is Poisson-binomial. Over the 600 random splits, the batch correction gave 55 violations for out-class compounds (56.2 expected; one-sided $p=0.58$) and 45 for in-class compounds (42.3; $p=0.35$). Per endpoint (Table~\ref{tab:tox21data}), the largest excess was SR-MMP out-class compounds, 9 of 50 against 4.6 ($p=0.039$ unadjusted; not significant after correction for 24 tests). In the first version of this paper, with other seeds and without tie-breaking, the out-class count was 74 of 600 against 56.6 expected ($p=0.011$). The present run, with fresh seeds, does not reproduce that excess, so it was most probably a chance finding; we know of no mechanism by which refitting the model could create an excess, because each split's violation probability given its fitted model is exactly the theoretical value. Calibrating on other scaffolds gave 67 and 44 violations in 240 scaffold splits, against 22.8 and 18.2 expected ($p<10^{-6}$).

\paragraph{Widths on real data.} For each random split, calibration-only intervals for a batch of 1000 were compared with audit-set intervals computed from the audit set of the same split, which is as large as the calibration set. Median width ratios per endpoint were 1.42--1.70 for correct rejections, 1.42--1.50 for false leads, 1.62--1.81 for confident hits, 1.50--1.82 for missed in-class compounds, 2.27--2.44 for deferred out-class compounds and 1.93--2.46 for deferred in-class compounds. The comparison is not budget-matched: it sets the calibration-only intervals against an audit set that would need as many extra labels as calibration.

\paragraph{Pilot samples (Table~\ref{tab:pilots}).} A request is infeasible when even the most conservative threshold ($u=1$) does not meet it, which at $\alpha=\delta=0.10$ happens with fewer than 22--23 calibration compounds of the class, for any batch size from 20 to 5000 (22 in the batch-free limit, since $1-0.9^{22}\ge0.9$). The software then uses the most conservative threshold, and the request is not certified. Violations among feasible pilots followed the exact law except for out-class compounds with pilots of 200 (11 of 65 against 5.1, $p=0.013$, one of six tests).

Designs (seeds $30000+1000d+r$): \emph{random}, room-temperature formulations split 50/25/25 into training, calibration and audit; \emph{system}, whole salt--solvent systems assigned to the three sets, with no row of a held system in training (one of 50 splits skipped for having fewer than five good calibration formulations); \emph{new salt}, the model trained on two thirds and calibrated on one third of the formulations of all salts other than LiPF$_6$, and audited on all 606 LiPF$_6$ formulations (154 good); \emph{pilot}, the same model calibrated on a random 40\% of the LiPF$_6$ formulations and audited on the rest. Scores were tie-broken at random. Thresholds used the batch correction at $\alpha=\delta=0.10$ with $m_y$ planned from the calibration prevalence and the audit-set size. Audit checks, the sweep ($12\times12$ requests) and the capacity rule (at most half the stand-in batch, chosen on one half with Bonferroni over 144 requests) followed the protocol of the audit check above, with 20 halvings per split. For the new salt, a median of 41 good formulations calibrated the good class, all requests were attainable, and coverage of the good class averaged 29\%; with the pilot, 62.5 good formulations calibrated it and coverage averaged 95\%. The poor-class excess with the pilot (8 against 4.0 expected) has one-sided Poisson-binomial $p=0.04$.



\paragraph{Real-score check of the batch law.} For each endpoint, one CatBoost model was trained on a random half of the labelled compounds. The other half was re-split 5000 times into calibration and test halves, with class counts held fixed; the batch law is conditional on class counts. Thresholds came from the batch correction ($\alpha=\delta=0.10$, $m_y$ = test compounds of class $y$). Ties were kept in this check; for the one-sided coverage event they can only make violations rarer. Table~\ref{tab:batchlaw} compares the observed fraction of re-splits in which per-class test coverage fell below 90\% with the exact Beta--Binomial value. Pooled over endpoints, the observed rates were 9.45\% against 9.35\% for out-class compounds ($z=0.79$, one-sided $p=0.22$) and 7.92\% against 7.89\% for in-class compounds ($z=0.30$, $p=0.38$). This check verifies the implementation and the law on real scores; it is not an explanation of chance variation in other runs. The largest per-endpoint deviations were NR-PPAR-$\gamma$ out-class compounds (10.58\% against 9.63\%), SR-ATAD5 out-class compounds (10.30\% against 9.52\%) and NR-ER in-class compounds (8.04\% against 9.80\%), among 24 comparisons. The first two are about two Monte Carlo standard errors (0.42 percentage points) above the exact value; the third is about four standard errors below it, in the conservative direction, as ties can make it.

\section{Dividing an audit from a batch by provenance}
\label{sec:source}

The electrolyte compilation records a DOI for every measurement, which makes a division by
source possible. Formulations were reduced to one row each as elsewhere, the room-temperature
window and the $8\,\mathrm{mS\,cm^{-1}}$ cut were applied unchanged, and 40\% of the
formulations were assigned to training, 20\% to calibration and the remaining 40\% to the
divisions below. The model was fitted on every temperature row of the training formulations,
with the standard representation and hyperparameters of this work (500 trees, depth 6,
learning rate 0.1), is therefore disjoint from the calibration set that sets its thresholds,
and was frozen before any division of the remainder. Ten repeats were run under seeds
$777+r$, with the tie-breaking jitter under $31337+r$. An earlier version of this experiment
fitted the model on its own calibration set, which voids the coverage guarantee for the
design and makes it incomparable with the rest of the work; every figure reported here is
from the corrected run, in which the losses are real but smaller.

The remainder was then divided three ways, the same model and calibration set serving all
three. \emph{Halved} splits it at random into an audit half and a stand-in batch, which is
the check used throughout the main text. \emph{By source} partitions the papers at random
into two groups and lets each formulation follow its own paper, so that no paper contributes
to both sides. \emph{One source out} takes a single paper's formulations as the batch and all
others as the audit, repeated over the five papers contributing at least 40 formulations to
the remainder, giving 41 runs over the ten repeats; because the inference is about papers, coverage is summarized with the paper as the unit, over the five per-paper means.

Coverage of the six consequence lines, with the repeat as the unit, was $0.97\pm0.04$ halved,
$0.70\pm0.12$ by source, and $0.77\pm0.11$ one source out with the paper as the unit over five per-paper means running from 0.58 to 0.90. Median audit-to-batch ratios of the
in-class rate were 1.02, 0.67 and 1.88, with per-repeat ratios spanning 0.32 to 2.24 and 0.36
to 8.14 in the two source divisions, so the division moves prevalence as well as the mapping
from score to outcome and the two cannot be separated here. Area under the curve on the batch
ranged from 0.81 to 1.00 across the source divisions, so the model's ranking does not
announce the failure either.

\section{Label-noise sensitivity of the solubility screen}
\label{sec:noise}

The solubility label is an indicator that the measured $\log S$ is at least $-4$, and AqSolDB
pools sources whose measurements of the same compound typically differ by 0.5 to 0.7 log
units. Taking 0.6 as the scale, the expected flip rate at the cut is 8 to 9\% among the
lipophilic compounds screened, 15\% of which lie within half a log unit of it. The sweep is
read at a resolution of the same order, so the analysis of
Section~\ref{main-sec:results:solubility} was repeated with the model, its hyperparameters,
its scores and the tie-breaking jitter held exactly as published, changing only the labels.

Seven labellings were compared over all ten splits, 110 evaluations in all. \emph{Base} is
the published labelling, which reproduces the published split-0 figures exactly (144
distinguished settings, 104 on the Pareto front, 26 certifiable, enrichment $2.40\times$ at
$p=1.8\times10^{-15}$) and so validates the harness. \emph{Cut} moves the threshold to
$-3.5$ and to $-4.5$. \emph{Random} flips 8\% of the compounds within half a log unit of the
cut, uniformly, under five seeds. \emph{Disagreement} flips the same number in decreasing
order of the recorded inter-source standard deviation, which is non-zero for 21.4\% of
compounds and gives a median flipped disagreement of 0.82 log units; this arm is asymmetric
by construction, 54 of 61 flips running from insoluble to soluble, and raises the screened
prevalence from 0.232 to 0.294. \emph{Structured} flips those the score ranks most extremely
against their class, and \emph{adverse} its mirror image, those it ranks least extremely.

The distinguished-setting count stays between 132 and 144 in every arm but the upward cut
shift, where its median falls to 120. The enrichment of the confident calls, with the setting
held at the published request, lies between 1.51 and 3.61 and clears the Bonferroni level
$0.05/144$ in all 100 evaluations where it is defined. The Pareto front holds its size, a
median 102 to 114 settings, while retaining a median 74 to 85\% of the base front's members
and 44\% in the worst split. The scenario degrades furthest: the median number of certifiable
settings falls from 26 to 18 (random), 2 (adverse) and 0 (disagreement, with six of ten
splits admitting none), and the cheapest certifiable budget rises from a median 134 per 1000
to 294, 380 and 378 respectively.

The structured arm is the one worth dwelling on, because it runs opposite to the mechanism of
Section~\ref{sec:surrogate}: flipping the compounds the score ranks most extremely raises the
certifiable count to 66 and the fold to 3.18 and lowers the budget to 36. Noise correlated
with the score makes the recorded labels agree with the model, which flatters every statistic
the screen reports. The damaging direction is the adverse one. Score-structured label noise
is therefore not ruinous in itself; its sign decides, and a proxy that agrees with the model
conceals rather than reveals.

\section{The DNA-encoded library screen}
\label{sec:del}

\paragraph{Data.} The library is BELKA,\citep{Quigley2024BELKA} released by Leash Biosciences through the Polaris record \texttt{leash-bio/belka-v1} under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. Each molecule is a trisynthon: one building block at each of three positions on a shared core, with the DNA tag replaced by a placeholder atom in the released SMILES. The release carries a binary binding call per molecule for three targets; we use soluble epoxide hydrolase. A sample of 1\,000\,000 distinct molecules was drawn from the 98\,807\,372 molecules that carry labels for all three targets, by ranking molecules on the MD5 digest of the UTF-8 bytes of the standardized SMILES, the byte \texttt{0x1F} and the decimal seed 20261028, and keeping the lowest million, so the sample is reproducible from the release and is not enriched for binders; the release's own \texttt{split\_group} partition was not used to choose molecules. In the million-molecule sample 7416 molecules bind the target, a rate of 0.74\%, and the first, second and third positions hold 306, 754 and 935 distinct blocks. The analysis runs on a further random subsample of 200\,000 of those molecules, drawn once under a fixed seed and used for every design and repeat; it holds 1442 binders, a rate of 0.72\%, and 294, 716 and 899 blocks at the three positions. In-class molecules are strongly concentrated by block: the five richest first-position blocks carry 60.5\% of the binders in the million-molecule draw and the richest binds at a rate of 0.709; in the 200\,000 analysed the same five carry 61.1\% and the richest binds at 0.685, against that sample's rate of 0.0072. The raw sequencing counts, replicates and controls live in a separate archive that was not downloaded, and the release's proprietary continuous scores were not used: the label is the released binary call. The release also carries its own held-out partition, tagging molecules \texttt{train}, \texttt{share} or \texttt{non\_share}; because the seeded million-molecule draw is almost entirely \texttt{train}-tagged, that partition plays no part here and the designs below are our own. A consequence worth stating is that the released binary call is itself derived from selection counts, which is the kind of proxy label Section~\ref{sec:surrogate} shows can be ruinous when its errors are structured with respect to a model's score; enrichment calls are known to favour particular physicochemical classes, which a fingerprint model will learn, so the coverage results of this library are statements about the recorded call and not about confirmed binding.

\paragraph{Why counts are not per 1000.} At an in-class rate below one percent a batch of 1000 contains about seven in-class molecules, and a setting that confirms a handful of hits then has a lower bound of zero whatever the audit size, so the outcome table carries no information. Intervals for this library are therefore quoted for the realized batch of each repeat, which runs to tens of thousands of candidates; the headline position-1 repeat has a batch of 42\,145. The scripts additionally scale each rate to a nominal batch of $10^5$ so that repeats can be compared, and those scaled figures are not the intervals quoted in the main text. The thresholds were set with the batch correction at a planned $m=1000$, inherited from the shared analysis code; the correction is conservative in $m$ and the deployed in-class order statistic was the 106th of 108, so the rule is more conservative than the request rather than less, but the mismatch between the planning scale and the reporting scale should be removed.

\paragraph{Splits over blocks, not molecules.} A molecule's nearest neighbour in a combinatorial library differs by one building block, so assigning molecules at random leaves a held-out molecule one substitution from a training molecule, and measures interpolation rather than transfer. Designs were therefore defined over blocks. In \emph{block1}, \emph{block2} and \emph{block3}, the distinct blocks at the named position are partitioned between training, calibration, audit and batch, so a batch molecule carries a block at that position that no training molecule carries. Holding out all three positions at once is stricter but discards the 91\% of molecules whose blocks disagree about their role and leaves too few in-class molecules to calibrate, so it is not reported. The \emph{random} design assigns molecules at random and is reported alongside to show how much the easier split flatters the result. The position-1 and position-2 designs were also run with a uniform random score through the identical pipeline, as the null the enrichment test must reject and as a check that coverage and interval width survive a worthless score.

\paragraph{The declared round.} A round was declared as a batch of which 30\% is built on first-position blocks that the model has not seen, and three preparations were compared, each with its own calibration and audit sets and all three sharing one fitted model per repeat. The naive and mirror arms are priced on one and the same batch; the pilot's batch draws its novel share from all of the new blocks rather than from the half the other two use, so it is a different draw in every repeat and between 1\% and 9\% smaller in four of the ten. The pilot arm also splits a fixed pool at the molecule level, which makes its calibration, audit and batch exchangeable by construction and its result a positive control. \emph{Naive} draws calibration and audit only from blocks the model trained on. \emph{Mirror} gives them the same 30\% novel fraction as the batch but draws that fraction from blocks disjoint from the batch's novel blocks, which is the position of a team that has measured some new chemistry and is about to make different new chemistry. \emph{Pilot} gives them the same novel fraction drawn from the batch's own novel blocks, split at the molecule level, which is a pilot plate of the chemistry the round will contain. Ten repeats were run for each, and the comparison in the main text is of the six consequence lines of the default setting.

\paragraph{The $\pm$ convention.} Coverage figures of the form $0.97\pm0.04$ are the mean over repeats plus or minus twice the standard error of that mean, with the repeat as the unit, not a standard deviation over repeats. Where a design contributes more than one run per repeat, the runs of a repeat are not independent and the repeat is still the unit.

\paragraph{Model, thresholds and repeats.} Features, model and hyperparameters are those used throughout (Section~\ref{sec:molecular}); the model is refitted once per repeat and frozen before any threshold is set. Scores were tie-broken at random before thresholds were taken, which matters here because many molecules receive identical scores. Thresholds used $\alpha=\delta=0.10$ with the batch correction, and audit intervals $\gamma=0.05$. The block holdouts ran ten repeats each and the random design nine. Runs are appended to disk as they complete, so a long campaign can be run in chunks.

\paragraph{The pre-flight check.} The label-free test of Section~\ref{main-sec:results:preflight} trains the same model class on fingerprints alone to separate one set of molecules from another, with the molecules of each set capped at 12\,000 and drawn at random, half for fitting and half for the reported area under the curve. Alongside it we report, for each of the three positions, the share of batch molecules whose block at that position also occurs in the comparison set. Three repeats were run per design.

The null is obtained by permutation. The audit and batch indices are pooled, split at random into two halves of the same sizes, and the same fit-and-score procedure is applied, twenty times per repeat at the same cap as the statistic itself; the statistic under that null is what a division with nothing to find returns at this sample size. Run on the three preparations of the declared round, over three repeats each, the audit-against-batch area under the curve was 0.657 (0.656--0.659) for the naive preparation, 0.660 (0.655--0.667) for the mirror and 0.500 (0.496--0.505) for the pilot, while the calibration-against-batch comparison gave 0.646, 0.744 and 0.496. The permutation null averaged 0.500 over the 120 replicates and spanned 0.470 to 0.524. The two preparations whose intervals fail are therefore separated from the null by a wide margin and the one that holds is not, at a block share of 0.70 in the failing arms rather than the 0.00 of the block holdouts, so this is the design on which the test is informative rather than determined by construction.

\section{Calibrating against a proxy label}
\label{sec:surrogate}

\paragraph{The shortfall as a gap between two conditional distributions.} Write $u$ for the frozen score, $y$ for the label a campaign cares about and $\tilde y$ for the proxy it calibrates against. The out-class threshold is the $k_0$-th order statistic of the scores of the calibration compounds with $\tilde y=0$, so it sits at the quantile $k_0/(n_0+1)$ of the score distribution conditioned on $\tilde y=0$, and that quantile is what the coverage request buys, up to the overshoot the batch correction introduces: $F(\tau_0\mid\tilde y=0)=\ell$, which exceeds $1-\alpha$ and which the control measures at 0.924 against a request of 0.90. Carrying $\ell$ rather than replacing it by $1-\alpha$ is what makes Eq.~\ref{main-eq:gap} of the main text a three-term decomposition rather than a subtraction. The coverage the campaign actually obtains is measured on the compounds with $y=0$, and is $F(\tau_0\mid y=0)$. Subtracting gives Eq.~\ref{main-eq:gap} of the main text. The in-class statement is the mirror image, with the survival function at the cut $1-\tau_1$ conditioned on $\tilde y=1$ and on $y=1$.

This is an accounting identity rather than a theorem, and that is the point of writing it down: it names the quantity a proxy has to control. The right-hand side involves the two conditional distributions only at $\tau_0$, so a proxy is free to disagree with the truth anywhere the score does not place a threshold, and the number of compounds it disagrees about enters nowhere. A proxy whose errors are independent of $u$ leaves both conditional distributions equal to the pooled one, up to the sampling error of the relabelled counts, and costs nothing. A proxy whose errors are concentrated at one end of the ranking shifts one distribution relative to the other and costs the difference.

\paragraph{Data.} Selection counts and measured inhibition constants for carbonic anhydrase II, IX and XII were taken from the CA-DEL release\citep{He2026CADEL} (Zenodo record 17656024, CC BY 4.0), whose affinity values derive from ChEMBL\citep{Zdrazil2024ChEMBL} and are distributed under CC BY-SA 3.0. No molecule in the release carries both a selection count and an affinity, so the two tables cannot be joined; the proxy studied here is therefore constructed from the affinities rather than taken from the selection counts, which is what allows the structure of its errors to be controlled while the compounds and the score are held fixed. Molecules were standardized as elsewhere in this work and merged on InChIKey. Compounds whose reported affinities span more than one log unit were excluded as inconsistent (121, 0 and 16 compounds), leaving 3844, 3103 and 2160 compounds for the three isoforms. In-class means $\mathrm{p}K_i\ge c$ for $c\in\{7,8\}$.

\paragraph{The frozen score.} A regressor was trained on the selection counts of the library molecules, with the logarithm of one plus the count as its target, a tenth of the molecules held out, and the same representation and hyperparameters as elsewhere. Its predictions on the affinity-labelled compounds were mapped to $(0,1)$ by a logistic transformation of the prediction standardized by the mean and standard deviation of the training molecules' predictions, so that no affinity enters the score. The score is frozen: it is the same in every condition of the experiment and does not depend on which proxy is under test. Its rank correlation with affinity is 0.22, 0.24 and 0.27 for the three isoforms, which is poor, and deliberately not repaired, because the experiment concerns the calibration label and not the model.

\paragraph{Proxy families.} Each repeat draws a proxy label vector over all compounds and then splits them 30/30/40 into calibration, audit and batch. Thresholds are set from the calibration compounds using the proxy's labels at $\alpha=\delta=0.10$ with the batch correction; the audit and the batch are then scored with the true labels. Three families are compared. \emph{Increasing in affinity} thresholds a strictly increasing function of the affinity at a cut chosen to match the true prevalence, which returns the true labels themselves and is the control; it did so in every repeat. \emph{Mistakes at random} relabels a fraction $f$ of the compounds of each class, chosen uniformly at random within the class. \emph{Mistakes by score} relabels the same number of compounds of each class, choosing within the out-class those the score ranks highest and within the in-class those it ranks lowest, with ties broken at random. Because the two relabel identically many compounds of each class, their confusion matrices against the truth are identical, and so are their sensitivity, specificity, area under the curve and implied prevalence; the prevalence shift agreed to machine precision in all 18 matched pairs. Twenty repeats were run per isoform, cut-off and condition, with seeds fixed as a function of those indices.

\paragraph{Results beyond Table~\ref{main-tab:surrogate}.} Taking the gap of Eq.~\ref{main-eq:gap} as measured in each run and adding it to the control's coverage predicts the coverage achieved to within 0.0040 on average and 0.0106 at worst over all 36 conditions, the two classes counted separately. Audit-set intervals covered 4797 of 5040 consequence lines, 95.2\% against a nominal 95\%, with no trend across the conditions. A rank correlation between the proxy and the affinity does separate the two families slightly, 0.458 against 0.496 at $f=0.20$ pooled, but in the direction opposite to the damage: the structured proxy correlates marginally better with affinity and covers twenty points worse.

\paragraph{Where in the ranking the mistakes sit.} A second sweep holds the relabelled share fixed at a tenth of each class and moves only the position from which those compounds are drawn. Within each class the compounds are sorted by score and a contiguous window of the required size is taken, centred on a target position in the within-class ranking; positions 0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.35, 0.50, 0.65, 0.80, 0.90 and 0.95 were used, at both cut-offs and all three isoforms, 20 repeats each. Because the window size does not depend on its position, the number of compounds relabelled in each class is the same in every condition: the measured disagreement with the truth was 0.100 in all 54 conditions, so sensitivity, specificity, area under the curve and implied prevalence are constant and only the location of the mistakes varies.

Averaged over isoforms and cut-offs, the mean absolute value of the gap of Eq.~\ref{main-eq:gap} was 0.036 for the out-class and 0.007 for the in-class when the window sat at positions 0.90--0.95, 0.007 and 0.036 when it sat at 0.05--0.10, and 0.009 and 0.007 when it sat between 0.35 and 0.80. Coverage shortfalls were 54 of 120 class-repeats for the out-class at position 0.95 and 54 of 120 for the in-class at position 0.05, against 6 to 19 of 120 at the intermediate positions. The dissociation is therefore by class as well as by position: a class is perturbed by mistakes near its own cut and not by mistakes near the other's. The sign of the perturbation is not fixed, because whether relabelling displaces a cut upwards or downwards depends on which compounds leave and join the class; over-coverage wastes budget where under-coverage breaks the guarantee, and both indicate that the cut is no longer where the request put it. Audit-set intervals covered 6247 of 6480 consequence lines, 96.4\%, with no trend across positions.

\paragraph{Monotone rescaling of the score.} That the outcome table depends on the score only through its ordering was checked on the library screen of Section~\ref{sec:molecular}. Ties were broken once on the original scale, and the whole analysis was then run on the model's probability of being in-class, on its logit, and on a potency-like scale obtained from the logit by an affine map composed with an inverse hyperbolic sine. All three gave outcome tables agreeing in every cell, with identical published intervals and identical verdicts, and on each scale both cut points were confirmed to be order statistics of the calibration scores of their own class. The consequence for reporting is that a threshold may be quoted on whichever scale a reader can interpret, a potency rather than a probability of a binary label, at no cost in coverage, provided the map is strictly increasing; and the mapping may be fitted on any data at all, including the calibration labels, because the rule never reads the values it produces.
