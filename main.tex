\begin{abstract}
Conformal prediction is advocated as a distribution-free form of uncertainty quantification: it returns prediction sets that contain the true label with a guaranteed probability, the coverage. Screening campaigns in chemistry and materials, including automated campaigns run in parallel batches, are planned on consequences instead: how many candidates receive a single label, how many of those calls are right, and how many must be assayed. We obtain these as counts, with prediction intervals valid in finite samples, for every coverage setting, taking the campaign rather than the compound as the unit of prediction. The two class-wise coverage requests index the available decision rules, and a labelled audit set, a random sample of the library to be screened, reveals what each one does. Before a batch is screened a team receives one line per outcome, each with an interval that holds with a specified probability under exchangeability alone and after a setting is chosen, and can choose under chance constraints on assays, budget or yield. Inverting the same intervals sizes a round: obtaining 20 hits at a 2\% in-class rate requires 2396 candidates rather than the 1000 an expectation suggests, and enlarging the audit from 49 to 384 compounds removes about 36\,000 syntheses from that requirement, which makes the audit the measurement with the largest effect on what a campaign costs. The audit must be built from the chemistry the batch will contain. In a DNA-encoded library screened for soluble epoxide hydrolase, reproducing a batch's composition without sharing its building blocks left coverage at 63\%, while sampling the batch's own building blocks restored 97\% at no cost in width. Sweeping the coverage requests for aqueous solubility traced about 140 distinct trade-offs, and cutting the measurement budget from 450 to 250 per 1000 compounds cost 73 false leads. On solubility, electrolytes and Tox21, 95--100\% of realized counts fell inside their 95\% intervals.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Conformal prediction has been advocated as a distribution-free form of uncertainty quantification.\citep{vovk2005algorithmic,AngelopoulosBates2023Gentle} It wraps any trained model, needs only a labelled calibration set, and returns for each new input a prediction set that contains the true label with probability at least $1-\alpha$ whenever calibration and future data are exchangeable. This guarantee, the coverage, requires no assumption about the model or the distribution of the data. That generality has made conformal prediction a standard tool for uncertainty quantification in machine learning and AI, and it is increasingly applied in chemistry and materials science.\citep{Norinder2014,CortesCiriano2019,Laghuvarapu2023CoDrug}

Here we apply conformal prediction to screening campaigns in chemistry and materials, in which a model decides which candidates go forward to a toxicity assay, a solubility or conductivity measurement, or synthesis. Increasingly such campaigns are run by automated and self-driving laboratories,\citep{Abolhasani2023SDL,Tom2024SDL} in which a model and a planner, rather than a person, decide what is made and measured. Because synthesis and characterization run in parallel, these campaigns proceed batch by batch: a model scores a candidate library and a rule decides what goes into the next plate or run, whose capacity is fixed. Unlike turn-by-turn optimization, a batch campaign often aims at covering a chemical or physical design space, mapping it or narrowing in on a set of optima (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). The decision, whether made by a team or by a planner, is the same, and so is the gap we address. In a binary screen each compound receives one of four prediction sets: $\{\text{out-class}\}$, $\{\text{in-class}\}$, both labels, or neither. A team acts on these sets. It advances or discards compounds that receive a single label and sends the others for an assay.

To choose among these actions, a team needs to know what they will do: how many compounds will receive a single label, how many of those calls will be right, and how many compounds will be sent for testing. These consequences, counted in compounds, are the terms in which screening decisions are made and budgets and capacities are planned. Coverage, the native coordinate of conformal prediction, is a statement about the prediction sets; it informs a decision once it is linked to these consequences. That link has to be measured. A set containing both labels is always correct but sends the compound to an assay, so two classifiers calibrated to the same coverage can sort a batch very differently.

Predictive uncertainty is ordinarily reported for one prediction at a time, and its aleatoric and epistemic parts are defined for a single query instance;\citep{Huellermeier2021Aleatoric} reviews of uncertainty quantification for drug design and property prediction follow that convention.\citep{Mervin2021UQ} Here the unit of prediction is the campaign rather than the compound. We report predictive uncertainty for the outcomes of a round, counted in compounds, and the same decomposition applies to it: the audit set reduces the uncertainty in the rates, and the size of the batch fixes the part that remains.

\paragraph{What we do and why.} We give coverage a specific role. The coverage level is a setting the team chooses, separately for the in-class and out-class compounds, and changing it changes which compounds are advanced, discarded or assayed. We therefore treat the coverage request as the control: sweeping it moves the classifier through the space of possible decisions. A labelled \emph{audit set}, a random sample of the library to be screened that was used neither for training nor for calibration, shows the consequences of each setting. From it we predict every screening outcome of a future batch, stated as counts of compounds, with prediction intervals that are valid in finite samples and require only the exchangeability that coverage already assumes.

These intervals are the deliverable, and obtaining them is what the method is for. Before a batch is screened, the audit states how many confident hits, false leads, missed in-class compounds and measurements the batch will contain, each as a count, equivalently a rate per 1000, with an interval that holds with a specified probability. The intervals are realistic in both directions: they hold at the stated level, where the usual shortcuts are too narrow, and they are no wider than the size of the audit requires. A team can therefore plan on them: book the assays, budget the measurements and expect the hits.

Coverage is thus the coordinate for navigating decisions, and the audited consequences are what a team chooses by. Choosing uses an objective, such as the expected cost or yield of the screen. It usually also uses guarantees: a plan the team can rely on, in which, with a stated probability, no batch needs more assays than the laboratory or the robot can run and every batch delivers enough hits to proceed. Such guarantees are bounds on counts that hold with a stated probability. The coverage requests reduce the navigation of these many consequences to two coordinates, in which a team can explore scenarios, each a set of such guarantees, and see which settings satisfy them.

Section~2 sets out the outcome table, the threshold correction, the navigation by coverage and the audit intervals that are the method's output; Section~3 validates the intervals in simulation and on real data and applies them to solubility, toxicity and electrolyte screens; Section~4 gives a workflow and the limitations.

\paragraph{Contributions.} The statistical tools are classical. What is new is how they are combined to answer the questions a screening team asks.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Predicted consequences with specified certainty.} For each setting, finite-sample valid prediction intervals and one-sided bounds for the future counts of confident hits, missed in-class compounds, false leads and deferred compounds follow from the audit set.\citep{Thatcher1964,Meeker2017Intervals} With a Bonferroni correction over the sweep they remain valid for whichever setting is chosen, so a setting can be chosen under chance constraints on assay capacity, budget or yield. We verify them in simulation and on three real datasets: toxicity (Tox21), aqueous solubility (AqSolDB) and electrolyte conductivity (CALiSol-23).
\item \emph{Coverage as the coordinate of decisions.} Every screening quantity is a sum of cells of a $2\times4$ table of prediction set by true label (Table~\ref{tab:outcomes}). With class probabilities, the class-wise coverage requests reach every two-threshold decision rule on the model score, so a sweep of the requests traces the decisions available to the team in two coordinates. Each setting induces a decision geometry of the same form as the cost-optimal reject rule, and the audited consequences show which costs it serves.
\item \emph{Sizing a round.} Inverting the same intervals answers the question asked before a campaign starts: how many candidates a batch needs for a target number of hits to be reached with a stated probability. This builds on established estimates of campaign hit counts from calibrated probabilities\citep{Buendia2019Hit} and extends them from an expectation to a bound. The distinction matters most where screens operate, since at low in-class rates the number of candidates required exceeds the expectation-based figure several-fold. The size of the audit enters that requirement directly, so audit measurements and synthesis trade against one another, and the trade speaks to the cost objection that led the expectation-based approach to forgo exchangeability in the first place. The exchange has a natural reading in the usual decomposition of predictive uncertainty:\citep{Huellermeier2021Aleatoric} the audit reduces the epistemic term, the uncertainty in the rates, while the size of the batch fixes the aleatoric term, which candidates happen to fall in it. The two are comparable when audit and batch are of similar size, so much larger audits buy little.
\item \emph{Matching the audit to the screen.} The intervals describe the future batch when the audit set comes from the library to be screened, and then remain correct even when the calibration does not match the screen. A labelled random pilot sample restores the coverage guarantees, and we give its required size. When scores are class probabilities, the calibration set can stand in for the audit set, at a quantified cost in width.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Related work.} Conformal prediction is established in cheminformatics, for toxicity, bioactivity, imbalanced data, applicability domain and drift.\citep{Norinder2014,NorinderBoyer2017,Sun2017Mondrian,Svensson2017,Bosc2019,CortesCiriano2019,Morger2021,Geylan2024,CPSign2024,Laghuvarapu2023CoDrug} The closest work estimates what a screening round will return. Buendia \textit{et al.}\ predict the number of hits an iteration of an iterative high-throughput screen will retrieve, by summing per-compound calibrated probabilities from Venn--ABERS predictors over the selected compounds, and show across six targets that the sum tracks the realized count;\citep{Buendia2019Hit,Buendia2018VAP} Svensson \textit{et al.}\ link conformal predictions to the gain of a screen.\citep{Svensson2018Gain} Estimating campaign-level counts is therefore established, and we build on it. A sum of calibrated probabilities is an expectation, and we add a probability statement about the realized count, which is what a plan that has to hold requires. Buendia \textit{et al.}\ also identify the obstacle to such a statement: selecting the highest-ranked compounds leaves the calibration and screened sets progressively less exchangeable, retaining validity would require measuring a fresh random subset of the screened library at every iteration, and the cost of those measurements would make the approach impractical. They therefore keep the initial calibration set, and report that the realized hit count falls outside their expected limits in later iterations. That randomly chosen, measured subset is the audit set used here, and its cost can be set against the compounds it saves (Section~\ref{sec:results:sizing}). These studies report coverage and efficiency, or a gain, on test data; we add prediction intervals for what a future batch will contain. In statistics, the coverage of a conformal predictor given its calibration set is known exactly,\citep{Vovk2012PAC,Bian2023TrainingConditional,Marques2025Beta} and related to tolerance regions and PAC prediction sets.\citep{Wilks1941,Park2020PAC} Risk-controlling methods\citep{bates2021distribution,Angelopoulos2024CRC,Angelopoulos2021LTT} choose thresholds to bound one chosen risk. Conformal selection\citep{JinCandes2023} selects candidates with false-discovery control, and set-valued classifiers with bounded class-wise error\citep{Sadinle2019} minimize ambiguity. Decision-theoretic work links prediction sets to actions. Conformal decision theory calibrates a decision parameter to a target risk,\citep{Lekeufack2024CDT} and Kiyani \textit{et al.}\ showed that prediction sets are a sufficient statistic for risk-averse decision makers with a known utility, for whom acting on the worst case within the set is optimal.\citep{Kiyani2025RAC} The question for which costs a classifier, or an abstaining classifier, is optimal goes back to the reject option\citep{Chow1970} and to ROC analysis.\citep{ProvostFawcett2001,Pietraszek2005} Our aim is complementary. We assume no utility, report \emph{all} screening outcomes of each coverage setting with valid intervals and bounds, and let a team choose afterwards under its own objective and constraints.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Setting and assumptions}
\label{sec:methods:setting}

We call the class with the property the screen looks for \emph{in-class} (label 1) and the other \emph{out-class} (label 0): toxic compounds in Tox21, soluble compounds in AqSolDB, and electrolytes that reach the conductivity target in CALiSol-23. A trained binary classifier is held fixed. It gives each compound a predicted probability of being in-class, $u=\hat p(\text{in-class}\mid x)$. A labelled \emph{calibration set} sets the thresholds. A labelled \emph{audit set} (in machine-learning terms, a held-out test set) is used only to measure what the thresholds do. A \emph{future batch} of $m$ compounds will be screened. We assume that calibration, audit and future compounds are exchangeable given the trained model, \textit{i.e.}\ their joint distribution is unchanged when they are reordered. This holds when all three are random samples of the library to be screened. The unit of sampling is the candidate to be screened: a compound, or for electrolytes a formulation, not an individual measurement. It fails when the batch comes from other chemical series or scaffolds, or is chosen using the model (Section~\ref{sec:results:dependence}). The probabilities need not be calibrated in the usual sense; only their ordering matters.

\subsection{Prediction sets and the outcome table}
\label{sec:methods:table}

We use class-conditional (Mondrian) split conformal prediction,\citep{vovk2005algorithmic} which controls coverage separately for the in-class and out-class compounds. The label \emph{out-class} is included in a compound's prediction set when $u\le\tau_0$, where $\tau_0$ is set from the out-class calibration compounds. The label \emph{in-class} is included when $u\ge1-\tau_1$, where $\tau_1$ is set from the in-class calibration compounds (Fig.~\ref{fig:sets}; ESI Section~\ref{esi-sec:notation}). Each compound then falls into one cell of a $2\times4$ table of true label by prediction set (Table~\ref{tab:outcomes}). Every screening quantity is a sum of cells:
\begin{itemize}
\item missed in-class compounds are in-class compounds labelled $\{\text{out-class}\}$;
\item false leads are out-class compounds labelled $\{\text{in-class}\}$, \textit{i.e.}\ compounds followed up that turn out out-class;
\item deferred compounds receive both labels or none;
\item coverage is the sum of the cells in which the true label is in the set;
\item the hit rate of the confident calls is a ratio of two cells.
\end{itemize}
The cells in each row add up to the number of compounds of that class. Moving a threshold therefore moves compounds between cells of the same row, so the outcomes trade off against each other. With class probabilities, a compound can receive both labels or none, but not both kinds for the same thresholds (ESI Section~\ref{esi-sec:geometry}). We report the two kinds separately, because an empty set signals a compound unlike the calibration data rather than an ambiguous one.\citep{Norinder2014}

\subsection{Coverage requests and thresholds}
\label{sec:methods:thresholds}

The standard threshold guarantees coverage of at least $1-\alpha$ only on average over calibration sets. With tens of in-class calibration compounds, the coverage of the one model actually deployed can fall well below $1-\alpha$. The coverage observed over a batch of $m_y$ future compounds of class $y$ follows a Beta--Binomial distribution that depends only on the number of calibration compounds of that class and on the chosen order statistic. This result requires only exchangeability.\citep{Marques2025Beta} We choose the least conservative threshold for which batch coverage is at least $1-\alpha_y$ with probability at least $1-\delta_y$, separately for each class. A coverage request therefore has four numbers, $(\alpha_0,\delta_0,\alpha_1,\delta_1)$. The guarantee is stated for a planned number $m_y$ of future compounds of each class, which we set from the batch size and the class proportions of the calibration set. In an automated laboratory the batch is a plate or one run of the platform, so $m$ is set by its capacity. The batch-free version,\citep{Vovk2012PAC} which follows a Beta law, controls coverage only for an infinitely large batch (Section~\ref{sec:results:thresholds}). As baselines we use the standard threshold and a one-sided correction based on the Dvoretzky--Kiefer--Wolfowitz inequality.\citep{Massart1990DKWM}

Tied scores, for example from identical fingerprints, keep the coverage guarantee conservative, but they can break the two-sided count intervals below, because tied compounds cross a threshold together. We therefore break ties at random: every compound receives an independent uniform number, and compounds are ordered by score and then by that number. This needs no knowledge of future scores and makes all the laws exact (ESI Section~\ref{esi-sec:correction}).

\subsection{Navigating decisions with the coverage request}
\label{sec:methods:choice}

\paragraph{Coverage as a coordinate.} With class probabilities, every rule that acts on single labels and defers the rest is a two-threshold rule on $u$: label out-class below one threshold, in-class above another, defer in between. The two coverage requests set the two thresholds, so a sweep of $(\alpha_0,\alpha_1)$ over a grid, at fixed $\delta$, traces the available two-threshold rules, up to the resolution of the calibration scores and of the grid. We keep one representative for each distinct pair of thresholds. Each setting maps to a vector of consequences, the counts of the outcome table and their bounds; the sweep traces a two-dimensional surface in this many-dimensional space, and its Pareto front over the outcomes that matter is the map on which a team explores its options.

\paragraph{The decision geometry of a setting.} Without stated costs, a setting together with the usual rule (act on single labels, defer the rest) induces a decision geometry: two thresholds on the model score. Given only the prediction sets, this rule is the risk-averse (max--min) optimal use of the sets for any costs in which a deferral is cheaper than either error (ESI Section~\ref{esi-sec:costs}).\citep{Kiyani2025RAC} Its geometry matches Chow's cost-optimal reject rule\citep{Chow1970} when the probability of being in-class increases with $u$, so each setting is expected-cost optimal for some region of cost ratios, possibly empty. Which costs a setting serves depends on the fractions of in-class compounds within each prediction set, which labelled data reveal; for a known utility, sets built for that utility can serve it better.\citep{Kiyani2025RAC} The coverage request therefore locates a decision, and its audited consequences evaluate it.

\paragraph{Objectives and chance constraints.} A team chooses a setting by an objective, such as the expected number of false leads, the expected cost or the expected yield, subject to constraints on what the campaign may consume or must deliver: at most $U$ assays, at most $U'$ missed in-class compounds, at least $L$ confirmed in-class compounds in a batch of $m$. Each constraint must hold with probability at least $1-\varepsilon$, so it is checked against a one-sided bound from the audit set, not against the expected count. This is a chance-constrained program\citep{CharnesCooper1959} with two decision variables, solved by enumerating the grid, in which the constraint probabilities are replaced by distribution-free bounds from the audit. A \emph{scenario} is such a set of constraints. Its feasible settings form a region of the coverage plane, and the objective selects a setting within it. When the objective and every constraint are monotone in the outcomes (fewer measurements, false leads or misses, and more confirmed compounds, are never worse), an optimal setting always lies on the Pareto front of the audited outcomes, taken over the quantities the objective and constraints use: a dominated setting is never better than the setting that dominates it and meets no constraint that setting fails. For every such objective, the table of Pareto settings with their expected counts and bounds is therefore a sufficient summary, and no cost ratio is needed to decide. Only an objective that is not monotone, such as a target workload that should be neither exceeded nor undershot, needs the full map. Changing the scenario, for example tightening the assay budget, moves or shrinks the region and shows what the change costs. An empty region is itself a result: the requirements cannot be guaranteed with the labelled data in hand, and either a constraint must be relaxed or more compounds labelled.

\subsection{Prediction intervals for the consequences}
\label{sec:methods:intervals}

\paragraph{What the method returns.} For a chosen setting and a planned batch of $m$ candidates, the output is one line per outcome of Table~\ref{tab:outcomes}: the expected count, and an interval that contains the realized count with a stated probability. Counts for a batch of 1000 are rates per 1000, so the same statement can be read either way. Tables~\ref{tab:solubility} and \ref{tab:tox21cells} show the form. Everything else in this paper is built from these intervals: the sweep compares settings by them, the scenarios constrain them, and the audit-size rule plans their width.

\paragraph{From the audit set.} Suppose an outcome (one cell, or a sum of cells) occurs $K$ times among $n$ audit compounds. Given the calibration set, the audit compounds and the $m$ future compounds are exchangeable. Given the total count, the number in the audit set is therefore hypergeometric, whatever the data distribution. Inverting this exact hypergeometric test gives conservative limits $[L,U]$ for the future count $K_m$ that hold with probability at least $1-\gamma$; they are quantiles of two Beta--Binomial distributions\citep{Thatcher1964} (ESI Section~\ref{esi-sec:intervals}). We use $\gamma=0.05$ throughout. The construction holds for any outcome, any score and any fixed decision rule; it does not depend on conformal prediction. In the language of quality control the audit set is an acceptance sample, and the limits are the classical tolerance limits for the number of defectives in a future lot.\citep{DodgeRomig1944,Wilks1941}

\paragraph{What the intervals cover.} The intervals cover the realized count in the future batch; no fixed underlying rate is assumed. They are conservative because counts are discrete. Each interval is valid for one outcome and one setting fixed before the audit set is seen. Choosing a setting by looking at its audited outcomes and then quoting its intervals is optimistic. Intervals computed at level $\gamma/J$ for each of the $J$ requests in the grid hold simultaneously, and therefore remain valid for whichever setting is chosen. For a scenario with $C$ bounded outcomes, each one-sided bound is computed at level $\varepsilon/(JC)$, so that every bound of every setting holds together with probability at least $1-\varepsilon$ and the chosen setting satisfies all constraints with at least that probability. Because the bounds hold jointly for all settings and all bounded outcomes, a team may pose any scenario over these outcomes, and change it, after looking at the map, without losing the guarantee. All intervals and bounds are conditional on the calibration set, that is, on the thresholds actually deployed.

\paragraph{What makes an interval useful.} Validity alone is attained by the trivial interval $[0,m]$, which contains every possible count of an outcome in a batch of $m$ and says nothing. An interval therefore earns its place by its width, and we report how many times narrower it is than that baseline, $m/(U-L)$, alongside coverage, following the principle of sharpness subject to calibration.\citep{Gneiting2007Sharpness} Width is set by the audit size and by how well the model ranks, and it can be planned before any labelling (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). Whether the rule is better than no model at all is a separate question about the rule, not about a future count, and we test it on the audit set.

\paragraph{From the calibration set alone.} With class probabilities and class-conditional thresholds, the calibration set can partly serve as its own audit. Whether an out-class compound receives the label \emph{in-class} depends only on the threshold set from the in-class calibration compounds, so for this event the out-class calibration compounds are exchangeable with future out-class compounds and the audit construction applies to them. Whether an out-class compound \emph{keeps} the label out-class is a coverage event, whose batch count follows the exact rank law used for the thresholds.\citep{Marques2025Beta} Each cell of a row is one of these two events or what remains after them, so computing both at level $1-\gamma/2$ bounds every cell of the row; a mixed batch adds an interval for the number of in-class compounds (ESI Section~\ref{esi-sec:calonly}). Evaluating a class's own threshold on the compounds that set it, as is done when ordinary rates are computed on the calibration set, is not allowed; it makes the counts too narrow (ESI Section~\ref{esi-sec:reuse}).

\subsection{Experiments}
\label{sec:methods:data}

\paragraph{Simulations.} Seven designs are described in ESI Section~\ref{esi-sec:sim}:
\begin{enumerate}
\item audit-set intervals, with $10^4$ replicates, each drawing its own data distribution;
\item finite libraries: 20 fixed libraries, each split at random 1000 times, which gives exchangeable data that are not independent;
\item threshold correction, with absolute Cauchy scores, $n=50$--500, $m=100$ and $10^6$ draws;
\item expected cost of the swept settings, computed exactly on the population, and selection: 4000 replicates of choosing among 100 settings under an assay capacity, with and without a Bonferroni correction;
\item scaffold dependence: compounds in clusters that share a shift of the predicted probability, with the future batch drawn from the calibration clusters or from new clusters;
\item tied scores: a cluster of identical scores at a threshold, with ties kept or broken at random;
\item calibration-only intervals: 3000 replicates per calibration size, compared with spending the same labelling budget half on calibration and half on an audit set.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Data.} Molecules were standardized with RDKit\citep{RDKit}, which keeps the largest fragment, neutralizes it and writes canonical SMILES; duplicates were then merged. One model was used throughout: Morgan fingerprints\citep{Rogers2010Morgan} (radius 2, 2048 bits) and CatBoost gradient-boosted trees\citep{Prokhorenkova2018CatBoost} (500 trees, depth 6, learning rate 0.1) with fixed hyperparameters. The experiments concern the coverage settings and their audited consequences for a given model, not the choice of model. For the twelve Tox21 endpoints,\citep{Huang2016Tox21,Mayr2016DeepTox} each endpoint was split 50/25/25 into training, calibration and audit sets: 50 times at random and 20 times by Bemis--Murcko scaffold.\citep{Bemis1996Murcko} Under the scaffold splits we also drew pilot samples of 200, 500 and 1000 compounds at random from the audit scaffolds, used each as the calibration set, and screened the remaining compounds. For AqSolDB,\citep{Sorkun2019AqSolDB} compounds with $\log S\ge-4$ were labelled soluble. Ten stratified 70/15/15 splits were made, the model was trained on all training compounds, and calibration and audit were restricted to $\log P>3.5$. Solubility most often limits lipophilic compounds, so a screen of this subpopulation is a realistic use; it is also a subpopulation on which the model's scores are distributed differently from the training data. For CALiSol-23,\citep{deBlasio2024CALiSol} a compilation of measured conductivities of lithium-salt electrolytes, each formulation (salt, concentration and solvent fractions) was labelled good if its conductivity at 20--30\,$^\circ$C was at least 8\,mS\,cm$^{-1}$. The same model was trained on all temperatures of the training formulations, with temperature, solvent fractions and fraction-weighted solvent descriptors as inputs, and scored each formulation at 25\,$^\circ$C. Four designs, 50 repeats each, drew calibration and audit sets from random formulations, from held-out salt--solvent systems, or, for a new salt, from the other salts or from a pilot sample of the new salt. To check the audit intervals on real data, each audit set was split at random, 20 times, into an audit half and a stand-in future batch. Details, counts and seeds are in ESI Section~\ref{esi-sec:molecular}.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Predicted counts hold at the stated certainty}
\label{sec:results:intervals}

The audit predicts the count of each outcome in a future batch, with an interval meant to hold with probability 95\%. In simulation, the realized count in a batch of 1000 compounds fell inside its interval in 95.7--98.9\% of replicates, for every outcome and every audit-set size (Fig.~\ref{fig:validation}). The intervals were also tight: for wrong single-label calls (false leads plus missed in-class compounds) they spanned about $\pm51$ per 1000 with 100 audit compounds and $\pm20$ with 1000 (ESI Table~\ref{esi-tab:validation}). The same held when fixed libraries were split at random, a design that is exchangeable but not independent. Averaged over libraries, coverage was 96.1--98.9\%, and no library fell below 94.7\% (Monte Carlo standard error 0.7\% per library; ESI Table~\ref{esi-tab:library}). The usual plug-in interval, Binomial quantiles at the audited rate, treats that rate as if it were known; it covered only 44--75\% of batches with 100 audit compounds and 84.5--93\% with 1000, and fell to 23\% for some libraries (ESI Tables~\ref{esi-tab:validation} and \ref{esi-tab:library}). The opposite shortcut, $m$ times the Clopper--Pearson interval for the rate, ignores the sampling variation of the future batch: it held with 100 audit compounds (96.3--98.6\%) but fell to 94.0--97.6\% with 300 and 86.6--94.3\% with 1000. Each shortcut omits one of the two sources of variation, the uncertainty of the audited rate and the variation of the batch, and fails where the omitted one dominates (ESI Section~\ref{esi-sec:sim}); the audit interval accounts for both. Scaled Clopper--Pearson intervals are therefore adequate only when the audit set is much smaller than the batch, and not for small batches such as a single plate. Bayesian posterior predictive intervals with a uniform prior were 1--6\% narrower and close to nominal in these designs, but carry no guarantee; in a two-stage design they fell to 94.8\% (ESI Table~\ref{esi-tab:twostage}). The audit interval held at its nominal level throughout. Width is what distinguishes these intervals from the trivial $[0,m]$: for a batch of 1000 the 95\% intervals of the screening outcomes spanned 16--109 counts in the real screens below, 9 to 63 times narrower than that baseline, and 36--204 after the joint correction over the sweep, 5 to 28 times narrower.

On real data the audit intervals behaved the same way (Table~\ref{tab:audit}). Splitting each Tox21 audit set into an audit half and a stand-in future batch, the intervals covered 95.6--99.0\% of the realized counts, for every outcome. Per endpoint, coverage was 95.8--97.1\% under random splits. It was 95.0--97.5\% under scaffold splits, in which the model had been calibrated on other scaffolds: the audit described the consequences correctly because it came from the compounds being screened. On AqSolDB, over all 132--144 settings of the sweep and all outcomes, the pointwise intervals covered 96.1\% of 216,960 realized counts. With the Bonferroni correction over the 144 requests, every interval of every setting covered its count together in 98.5\% of 200 audit--future splits.

\subsection{Sizing a round}
\label{sec:results:sizing}

A campaign asks the audit's question in reverse. A project needs a certain number of hits to continue, and what it must decide is how many candidates to put in the batch. The smallest batch whose lower bound on confident hits reaches the target answers that, and it follows from the same intervals by a search over the batch size.

The answer differs from the one an expectation gives, and the difference is largest where screens operate (Fig.~\ref{fig:sizing}, left). Dividing the target by the observed in-class rate, which is what a sum of calibrated probabilities amounts to,\citep{Buendia2019Hit} asks for 1000 candidates to obtain 20 hits at a rate of 2\%. The batch that delivers 20 hits with probability 0.95 holds 2396 candidates when the rate was measured on an audit of 384, and 1867 on an audit of 1000. At a rate of 50\% the two answers are 40 and 55. The gap closes as the rate rises, because what the bound carries is the uncertainty in the rate itself, and that matters least when the rate is high and the audit has seen many in-class compounds.

The size of the audit enters the requirement directly, which prices audit measurements against synthesis. At a rate of 2\%, an audit of 49 compounds leaves the required batch at 38\,693 candidates and an audit of 384 at 2396, so 335 further audit measurements remove about 36\,000 syntheses from the requirement; at a rate of 10\% the same 335 measurements remove 287. On this accounting the audit is not an overhead on the campaign but the measurement with the largest effect on what the campaign costs, and its effect is largest exactly where a screen's hit rate is low.

The exchange has a limit (Fig.~\ref{fig:sizing}, right). An interval's width carries one term in the audit size and one in the batch size (Section~\ref{sec:methods:intervals}): the first is the uncertainty in the rate, which the audit reduces, and the second is which candidates happen to fall in this particular batch, which it cannot. The two contribute equally when audit and batch are of the same size, and beyond that the batch term dominates, so an audit much larger than the batch buys little. For a batch of 50, an audit of 400 already leaves 89\% of the interval's variance attributable to the batch.

Re-estimating between rounds costs little. A campaign that screens a first batch, revises the rate and sizes the next has looked at the data more than once, which a fixed-sample interval does not cover; spending the confidence level across a declared number of looks restores validity. Three looks rather than one raise the required batch from 335 to 375 candidates at a rate of 10\%, and from 2396 to 2983 at 2\%. The option to stop early, or to continue on a revised estimate, therefore costs between a tenth and a quarter of a batch.

\subsection{What a measurement budget costs: a solubility screen}
\label{sec:results:solubility}

After standardization and de-duplication, 9254 AqSolDB compounds remained, 2467 of them with $\log P>3.5$. In each of ten splits, the lipophilic calibration and audit sets held about 370 compounds each, 22\% of them soluble ($\log S\ge-4$). Audit AUROC was 0.91--0.93 overall and 0.82--0.88 within the lipophilic subset.

\paragraph{The price of a budget.} Cutting the measurement budget from 450 to 250 per 1000 lipophilic compounds cost 73 additional false leads, with all requirements guaranteed jointly at 95\%, and no setting could be guaranteed below 200 measurements per 1000. At this audit size the guarantee itself cost about 110 measurements per 1000: the chosen setting expects 286 and is bounded at 395. The rest of this section shows how these numbers follow from the map.

\paragraph{The decision space.} Sweeping $(\alpha_0,\alpha_1)$ over 12 values each, with $\delta=0.10$, gave 132--144 distinct settings per split. Fig.~\ref{fig:solubility} (left) shows their audited consequences for one split: one model, one endpoint, and outcomes ranging from 10 missed soluble compounds per 1000 with a third of the batch sent to measurement, to almost no measurements at the price of about 50 missed soluble compounds. Of the settings, 74--120 per split were Pareto-optimal for missed soluble compounds, false leads and measurements (ESI Table~\ref{esi-tab:aqsolsplits}), so the front is the menu, and the team's constraints make the choice. Table~\ref{tab:solubility} gives the audited consequences of the two extremes, with intervals for a batch of 1000. Setting A misses 10 soluble compounds per 1000 (interval 2--29) and sends 335 to measurement; setting B sends none to measurement and misses 52 (29--84). Because A and B were picked by looking at the audit, the Bonferroni intervals in Table~\ref{tab:solubility} are the ones that hold for them. Over the ten splits the corresponding audited values were 0--20 missed and 203--522 deferred for setting A, and 26--61 missed and 0--42 deferred for setting B.

\paragraph{Scenarios.} Two scenarios illustrate the choice (Fig.~\ref{fig:solubility}, centre and right). In both, a batch of 1000 lipophilic compounds must yield at least 60 confirmed soluble compounds and miss at most 90, and the objective is the fewest expected false leads. In the first, at most 450 compounds may be sent to measurement; in the second, at most 250. The limits were set after inspecting the map, which the joint bounds allow. Every constraint was checked against a one-sided audit bound, with the Bonferroni correction over the 144 requests and the four bounded outcomes, so that all constraints hold together with probability at least 95\%. Under the first budget, 26 of the 144 requests were feasible; the chosen setting expects 115 false leads, 286 measurements and 23 missed soluble compounds per 1000, with bounds, holding together with probability at least 95\%, of 395 measurements, 75 misses and at least 66 confirmed. Tightening the budget to 250 left 9 feasible requests and moved the choice to a setting expecting 188 false leads: the tighter budget costs 73 additional false leads per 1000. Choosing instead by the upper bound on false leads, a risk-averse objective, selected the same settings in both scenarios. Both objectives are monotone, and as expected every chosen setting lay on the Pareto front of the four bounded outcomes, which held 105 of the 144 settings (ESI Section~\ref{esi-sec:costs}); the Pareto table is a complete menu for such objectives, and the constraints select from it. Over a range of budgets the price of the budget can be read off directly: the best feasible setting expected 221 false leads at 200 measurements per 1000, 188 at 250--300 and 133 at 350, and beyond 400 the budget no longer constrained the choice (115 false leads). Below 200 the feasible region was empty: no setting met all requirements with the labels in hand, so a team must relax a requirement or label more compounds. Against the trivial interval $[0,1000]$, which validity alone attains, the chosen setting's 95\% intervals were 9 to 26 times narrower for the four constrained outcomes, and 5 to 14 times narrower after the joint correction over the sweep. The advanced compounds, those given a single in-class label with no measurement spent, were 54\% soluble against a prevalence of 22\%, an enrichment of 2.4 times that is significant on the audit set after a Bonferroni correction over the 144 settings (one-sided Fisher exact test, $p=2\times10^{-15}$), so the model demonstrably earned the measurements it saved. The gap between expected and bounded counts (286 against 395 measurements) is the price of the guarantee at this audit size, and labelling buys it down: at the same audited rate, half the audit (192 compounds) would give a bound of 432, twice the audit 372 and four times 358. A capacity rule chosen this way on one half of the audit set and checked on the other half never exceeded capacity in 200 splits (median upper bound 484, realized 304 measurements per 1000). No cost ratio is needed for this choice; the cost ratios for which each setting is expected-cost optimal are mapped in ESI Fig.~\ref{esi-fig:costmap}.

\subsection{Auditing a new salt: an electrolyte screen}
\label{sec:results:calisol}

CALiSol-23 holds 13,825 conductivity measurements of lithium-salt electrolytes from 27 publications.\citep{deBlasio2024CALiSol} After removing 523 rows without a conductivity and 23 rows from one source whose values lie about 100 times below all others for the same formulations (a probable unit error), 1495 formulations had been measured at room temperature, 275 of them (18\%) at or above 8\,mS\,cm$^{-1}$. The threshold is close to the conductivity of standard carbonate electrolytes; 1\,M LiPF$_6$ in EC/EMC (3:7) measures 9.4--9.5\,mS\,cm$^{-1}$ in this dataset, so a good formulation is one within reach of that standard. A threshold of 10\,mS\,cm$^{-1}$ would leave about 18 good formulations in a calibration set of 350, too few for the request to be attainable (Section~\ref{sec:results:dependence}). Measuring conductivity is cheap compared with building and cycling cells, so here a deferral means a measurement.

\paragraph{A new salt.} The sharpest test is a new salt. With a model trained and calibrated on the other salts and the 606 LiPF$_6$ formulations screened, coverage of the good class averaged 29\% and fell below 90\% in all 50 splits (Table~\ref{tab:calisol}; Fig.~\ref{fig:calisol}, right). The audit of the screened formulations showed what this meant for the screen: at the default setting it reported a median of 174 missed good electrolytes per 1000 formulations, about two-thirds of the good ones, and 59 confident hits. Calibrating instead on a random pilot of 40\% of the LiPF$_6$ formulations, about 240 measurements, restored the coverage guarantee (5 shortfalls against 3.3 expected for the good class; 8 against 4.0 for the poor class), and the audit showed the gain: 10 missed good electrolytes and 159 confident hits per 1000, for 308 measurements. Formulations split at random over all salts met the request (2 and 4 shortfalls against 4.7 and 3.1 expected), and the sweep of coverage requests gave 108--132 distinct settings per split, trading measurements (0--230 per 1000) against false leads (0--150) and missed good electrolytes (0--30) (Fig.~\ref{fig:calisol}, left). Holding out whole salt--solvent systems also broke the coverage guarantee (20 and 22 shortfalls against 4.5 and 5.0).

\paragraph{The audit carries the guarantee.} In all four designs, including the two in which the coverage guarantee failed, an audit set from the screened formulations reported the consequences correctly. Audit intervals for the default setting covered 95.5--100\% of realized counts, pointwise intervals over the whole sweep 97.2--97.6\%, and with the Bonferroni correction every setting and outcome was covered together in 98.4--99.2\% of audit--future splits. A capacity rule chosen on one audit half, as a planner would choose it, never exceeded capacity on the other half, in 980--1000 checks per design. The intervals were also informative: for the scenario setting the 95\% intervals were 12 to 63 times narrower than the trivial $[0,1000]$, and 7 to 28 times narrower jointly over the sweep. The advanced formulations were 71\% good against a prevalence of 15\%, an enrichment of 4.7 times (one-sided Fisher exact test on the audit set, $p=2\times10^{-37}$, Bonferroni over 144 settings). Scenario maps for the electrolyte screen behave as for solubility (ESI Fig.~\ref{esi-fig:calisolscen}). Intervals from the calibration set alone covered 99.7\% of counts for random formulations; for a new salt they rely on a calibration that does not match, and a separate audit is the tool to use.

\paragraph{Per plate.} A self-driving platform measures plates rather than batches of 1000. For the pilot-calibrated rule, an audit of 364 formulations gives, for one 96-well plate, 30 expected measurements with a 95\% interval of 20--40; scaled Clopper--Pearson would give 25--35, too narrow at this ratio of audit to batch size (Section~\ref{sec:results:intervals}). The one-sided bound of 38 measurements per plate holds with probability 95\% for a single plate, and a bound of 43 holds for all ten plates of a campaign together (level 0.5\% per plate).

\subsection{Building the audit for the round it prices: a DNA-encoded library}
\label{sec:results:del}

The electrolyte screen showed that an audit drawn from the screened population bounds the consequences where a calibration set from elsewhere does not. A campaign poses a harder version of the same question, because the next round is often not drawn from the population already screened: it introduces chemistry that has not been made. What the audit must then contain is a question about its design, and a DNA-encoded library answers it cleanly, because the chemistry is built from a catalogue of building blocks and a round can be declared in those terms.

How much is at stake can be measured in such a library by withholding blocks. Assigning molecules to training, calibration, audit and batch at random keeps all four exchangeable, and the intervals then covered 53 of 54 consequence lines over nine repeats, at their nominal level. Withholding instead every molecule carrying a given block at one position, so that the batch is built on chemistry absent from the calibration and audit sets, breaks them: over ten repeats each, coverage fell to 48\% when the withheld position was the third, 37\% when it was the second and 35\% when it was the first. Nothing in the model announces this. Its ranking of the batch stayed strong throughout, between 0.71 and 0.98 by area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, and the enrichment of its confident calls over the base rate was significant in every repeat of every design. In the worst position-1 repeat a batch was promised between 578 and 723 confident hits, at a model AUROC of 0.89, and returned 119.

We use a sample of a published library screened against soluble epoxide hydrolase, in which each molecule is assembled from one block at each of three positions and carries the library's own binding call; the in-class rate is 0.7\%. A round is declared in which 30\% of the batch is built on blocks at the first position that the model has never seen. Three ways of preparing for that round are compared, each supplying its own calibration and audit sets, all three sharing one model per repeat so that nothing differs between them but the sets themselves: calibration and audit drawn only from blocks the model trained on, as a team would do by habit; drawn with the same 30\% share of new chemistry as the batch but from different new blocks, which is the position of a team that has measured some new chemistry and is about to make different new chemistry; and drawn with the same share from the batch's own new blocks, which is a pilot of the chemistry the round will contain.

Reproducing the batch's composition is worth nothing. Over ten repeats, the six consequence lines were covered 38 times out of 60 when calibration and audit held only familiar blocks, and 38 times out of 60 when they carried the same proportion of new chemistry drawn from other blocks: the two are indistinguishable, and both fall far below the nominal 95\%. Sampling the batch's own blocks covered 58 of 60, at the nominal level. The pilot was also the sharpest of the three, its interval on confident hits spanning 70 candidates against 77 and 76, so the valid interval cost nothing in width here.

What distinguishes the three is therefore not how much new chemistry the audit contains but whether it contains \emph{that} chemistry. The distinction matters because the first is cheap and can be arranged before synthesis, while the second requires measuring a sample of the new blocks, which is an experiment. Where a round introduces chemistry the audit does not hold, the guarantee is bought with that experiment or not at all.

\subsection{What the audit must sample}
\label{sec:results:dependence}

The audit describes a future batch drawn from the same library as the audit itself. In simulation, when compounds share scaffold-level shifts of their predicted probability, the intervals held when the audit sampled the scaffolds being screened: coverage was 95.4--97.9\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:scaffold}, left). Auditing other scaffolds does not substitute. When the future batch came from new scaffolds with a scaffold effect of one standard deviation on the logit scale, coverage fell to 71\% for false leads, 83\% for missed in-class compounds and 88\% for deferrals; with 1.5 standard deviations it fell to 62--75\%.

\paragraph{Tox21 scaffolds.} On Tox21 scaffold splits, calibration on other scaffolds broke the coverage guarantee: per-class coverage fell short in 28\% of splits for out-class compounds and 18\% for in-class compounds, two to three times the exact-law expectation (22.8 and 18.2 of 240 splits). An audit set from the screened scaffolds still reported the consequences correctly (Table~\ref{tab:audit}). Calibrating instead on a random pilot sample of the screened scaffolds restored the coverage guarantee: per-class violations then followed the exact law (ESI Table~\ref{esi-tab:pilots}).

\paragraph{Solubility.} We also calibrated on all calibration compounds (about 1390) and then screened lipophilic compounds. Coverage of the soluble class among lipophilic audit compounds averaged 72\% and fell below the requested 90\% in all ten splits. Calibrating on the lipophilic compounds only gave 94\% (below 90\% in one split of ten, as the request allows), at the price of more measurements: 462 rather than 209 per 1000.

\paragraph{How large a pilot must be.} In the Tox21 pilots, violations for the in-class compounds were nonetheless frequent, 29\%, 13\% and 12\% for pilots of 200, 500 and 1000 compounds, against 26\%, 12\% and 9\% expected, because many pilots held too few in-class compounds for the request to be attainable. At $\alpha=\delta=0.10$ at least 22--23 calibration compounds of a class are needed, whatever the batch size; 70\%, 41\% and 11\% of the pilots had fewer (median 13, 29 and 54 in-class compounds). A pilot therefore needs about $22/\pi$ random compounds at prevalence $\pi$, about 770 for NR-PPAR-$\gamma$. At that minimum the request is met only by the most conservative threshold; each further labelled compound then buys single-label calls.

\subsection{Using the calibration set as its own audit}
\label{sec:results:calonly}

With class probabilities, intervals from the calibration set alone covered the future count of every cell in 97.7--100\% of simulation replicates, counting only future compounds of the cell's class, for 300 and 1000 calibration compounds; for a mixed batch, all cells were covered together in 99.5\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:calonly}). They covered 98.7\% of realized audit counts on Tox21 random splits, all 8136 checked counts on AqSolDB, and 99.7\% on CALiSol-23 random splits. The price is width. With a fixed budget of 1000 labelled compounds, used entirely for calibration or split equally between calibration and an audit set, calibration-only intervals were 21--28\% wider for false leads and missed in-class compounds, 31--39\% wider for confident hits and correct rejections, and about twice as wide for deferrals (ESI Table~\ref{esi-tab:calonly}). On Tox21, against an audit set as large as the calibration set, they were 1.4--1.8 times as wide for single-label outcomes and 1.9--2.5 times for deferrals. Calibrating on other scaffolds reduced their coverage to 74\%, and calibrating on other salts to 33\%. The calibration set can therefore stand in for the audit set when labels are scarce, but a separate audit set from the screened library gives sharper and more robust consequences.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The audit turns a conformal classifier into a forecast: before a batch is screened, it predicts how many hits, false leads, misses and measurements the batch will contain, with intervals that hold at a stated probability. Coverage serves as the coordinate a screening team steers by. The two class-wise coverage requests move a conformal classifier through the decisions available to a screening team, and for every setting the consequences are counts in one outcome table: compounds advanced, discarded and deferred, and how many of those calls are wrong. Linked to these counts by the audit, coverage becomes a coordinate a team can steer by. An objective, usually with guarantees on what a campaign will consume and deliver that hold with a stated probability, decides where to steer. The coverage plane is where such scenarios are explored, and an audit set from the library to be screened supplies the consequences, with finite-sample valid intervals that remain valid after the team has chosen a setting by looking at them. On Tox21, AqSolDB and CALiSol-23 the intervals held at their nominal level, also when the calibration came from other scaffolds or other salts, because the audit came from the screened compounds.

\paragraph{Using the method in a screen.} The results suggest the following workflow:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Label a random sample of the library to be screened and use it as the calibration set. It must contain at least 22--23 compounds of the rarer class for a request at $\alpha=\delta=0.10$ to be attainable, and more for a useful one: keep labelling randomly chosen compounds, about $22/\pi$ or more at prevalence $\pi$. Choosing compounds by their score to find the rare class faster breaks exchangeability unless the selection is accounted for.\citep{BarberBeyond2023}
\item Label a second random sample as the audit set. Its size sets the width of the intervals: for an outcome with frequency $p$ in a batch of $m$, $n$ audit compounds give a 95\% interval of roughly $\pm2m\sqrt{p(1-p)(1/n+1/m)}$, about $\pm35$ per 1000 for $p=0.1$ and $n=400$ (ESI Section~\ref{esi-sec:intervals}). Choose $n$ from the width the decision needs, not from validity, which any audit size delivers. With class probabilities and few labels, the calibration set can serve instead, at a cost in width.
\item Draw the audit from the chemistry the next batch will contain. Where a batch introduces building blocks, scaffolds or series the audit does not hold, matching its composition is not enough: an audit carrying the same proportion of new chemistry drawn from different blocks performed no better than one ignoring the shift, while an audit sampling the batch's own blocks recovered the nominal level (Section~\ref{sec:results:del}). If part of the next batch is novel in this sense, a pilot of that chemistry is what restores the guarantee.
\item Sweep the coverage requests and audit each setting: intervals for missed in-class compounds, false leads and deferrals in the planned batch, with a Bonferroni correction over the sweep.
\item State a scenario, an objective and the limits that must hold with probability at least $1-\varepsilon$ (assay capacity, budget, minimum yield), and choose the best setting among those whose audit bounds satisfy the limits. If the objective and limits are monotone in the outcomes, the table of Pareto settings is all the team needs to see.
\item Screen, and compare the realized counts with the predicted intervals as a check on exchangeability.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{The audit decides which questions can be asked.} Calibration and audit sets are usually treated as machinery, chosen for size and then forgotten. They are better understood as the statement of what the guarantees are about, because a sample licenses claims about the population it was drawn from and about nothing else. How the audit is built therefore fixes, before any model is fitted, which questions a campaign can answer, and three choices in building it each foreclose something. The population fixes the domain: an audit drawn from the top of a ranking prices further batches drawn from the top of that same ranking, and says nothing about compounds further down, which is where a campaign goes once the top is exhausted. The unit fixes what a count means: an audit sampled one compound per series estimates the rate at which series are real and prices a batch of new series, not a batch of analogues of a few confirmed ones, whose effective size is the number of series it contains. The chemistry fixes the reach: an audit holding only building blocks already screened prices batches built from those blocks, and carrying the same proportion of new chemistry as the batch without sampling that chemistry is not a substitute (Section~\ref{sec:results:del}). The score belongs to the same declaration, since thresholds and the sets they define move when the model is refitted, so an audit describes the deployment it was measured under.

Two consequences follow for practice. The question should be settled before the audit is built, because an audit assembled for one question usually cannot be repurposed for another; a campaign that discovers halfway through that it needs to speak about a different population has to measure again. And because an audit is also an option on the questions a campaign may ask later, it is worth spending part of it on compounds a project would not otherwise measure, lower-ranked compounds or a few members of each of several series, so that when the obvious batch is exhausted the next question can still be answered. Diversity in an audit is not a statistical nicety but the purchase of future questions.

\paragraph{Campaigns run batch by batch.} Automated and self-driving laboratories synthesize and characterize in parallel, so their campaigns are sequences of batches, each with a fixed capacity, and the framework applies to each batch as it stands. Batch campaigns also tend to pursue different goals from turn-by-turn Bayesian optimization, which proposes one experiment at a time in search of a single optimum. Because a batch is evaluated in parallel, its members are chosen to be diverse,\citep{Gonzalez2016LP,HernandezLobato2017TS,Shields2021EDBO} and the campaign often seeks coverage of a chemical or physical design space: mapping a landscape, as in space-filling designs and closed-loop phase mapping,\citep{Joseph2016SFD,Kusne2020CAMEO} contracting onto promising regions,\citep{Eriksson2019TuRBO} or finding a set of optima such as a Pareto front.\citep{Daulton2020qEHVI} In each of these modes a batch is a selection from a scored library under a fixed capacity, and its consequences are what the audit predicts. The batch size $m$ is the plate or run size, and the scenario constraints are the capacity of the platform, the reagent or instrument budget per run and the minimum yield needed to proceed. Choosing which candidates enter the batch is the screening decision itself and is accounted for in the outcome table. When the model is retrained between batches, the thresholds change, so the calibration, the audit and the map are redone for the new deployment. Two practices follow. First, reserving a small random audit slice in every batch, measured whatever the rule decided, checks continually that the screened library still matches the calibration data; it is the acceptance sample of quality control applied to each lot. Second, the guarantee holds per batch with probability $1-\varepsilon$; a statement over all $K$ batches of a campaign needs level $\varepsilon/K$ for each. Because the audit bounds are stated in counts, they can be checked automatically, and the Pareto table is a compact menu from which a planner, including an AI agent, can choose a setting without a cost ratio being stated; we do not test such a planner here.

\paragraph{Limitations.} All guarantees rest on exchangeability between the calibration, audit and screened compounds. A change of chemical series, scaffold or assay breaks it, as the scaffold results show. So does a batch selected with the model, as in active learning, unless the selection is part of the design, and so does a library proposed by a generative model or extended into new chemistry, as the new-salt screen shows;\citep{Fannjiang2022FeedbackCovariateShift,BarberBeyond2023} methods for shifted data are complementary. Compounds that share scaffolds are correlated, so the effective size of a pilot or audit sample is closer to the number of scaffolds sampled, and the same holds for the batch: an audit drawn one compound per series prices a batch of new series, not a batch of analogues of a few confirmed ones, whose effective size is the number of series it contains. The pools used here are diverse enough for this to be immaterial, with 328 similarity clusters among 384 audited solubility compounds and over 1200 among the Tox21 compounds, but it governs libraries built combinatorially or around a single target. A structural check does not substitute for a pilot. We found no relation between how well an interval covered and how similar the held-out building blocks were to those the audit held; blocks indistinguishable by fingerprint gave the lowest coverage of all, being stereoisomers whose in-class rates differ severalfold, so similarity measured on an achiral representation cannot certify a batch that differs stereochemically from its audit. Each interval covers one outcome of one setting; statements about many outcomes or settings need the Bonferroni correction, which widens intervals; with 144 requests and four bounded outcomes, the joint guarantee cost about 110 measurements per 1000 at an audit of 384 compounds. Larger audits lower this price, and bounds that exploit the monotone structure of the sweep could lower it further. The sweep reaches every two-threshold rule only with class probabilities, and the cost-optimal rule is a two-threshold rule only if the probability of being in-class increases with the score. Cost ratios are rarely known precisely, so we report the range of ratios for which a setting is cheapest. AqSolDB combines measurements from many sources, with a typical spread of 0.5--0.7 log units between sources;\citep{Sorkun2019AqSolDB} labels near the cut-off at $\log S=-4$ are therefore noisy. CALiSol-23 likewise pools 27 sources, and several formulations were measured at many temperatures or concentrations, so neighbouring formulations are correlated. The guarantees refer to the labels as recorded. Finally, we treat binary endpoints only, and one model throughout.

\section{Conclusions}

The coverage level of a conformal classifier is a coordinate for navigating screening decisions, and the audited consequences, counted in compounds and bounded with a stated probability, are what a team chooses by under its own objective and guarantees. Each setting sorts a future batch into one table of prediction set by true label, and every screening quantity is a sum of its cells. A labelled audit set from the library to be screened predicts all of these counts for the next batch, with intervals that hold with a specified probability in finite samples, under exchangeability alone and after a setting is chosen. The same request led to very different consequences across twelve Tox21 endpoints, and one model offered more than a hundred distinct trade-offs for a solubility screen and for an electrolyte screen. When the calibration did not match the screened population, as for a new lithium salt, the audit still bounded the consequences correctly. In automated campaigns, which run in parallel batches, the same construction applies to each batch, and the bounds become capacity constraints a planner can check. Inverted, the same intervals size a round: the batch that delivers a target number of hits with a stated probability is larger than dividing the target by the rate suggests, by a factor that grows as the rate falls, and the audit that pins the rate down is the measurement with the largest effect on what the round costs. What the audit must contain is decided by the batch it prices, since an audit that reproduces a batch's composition without sampling its chemistry prices nothing. With an audit built this way, a team sees those consequences before the campaign starts.
